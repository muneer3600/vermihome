Best Worms for Composting: Pick the Right Species

Published October 15, 20266 min read

The best worms for composting are red wigglers (Eisenia fetida). They live near the surface, tolerate normal indoor temperatures, reproduce quickly, and eat roughly half their body weight in food scraps every day. European nightcrawlers are a solid second choice — but the earthworms from your garden will not work in a bin.

What makes a worm a good composter?

Not every worm wants the job. The species that thrive in worm bins share a few traits: they live in the top layers of decaying organic matter rather than deep soil, they tolerate being close together, and they reproduce fast enough to keep the population going.

Scientists call these surface-dwellers “epigeic” worms. Red wigglers are the classic example. Deep-burrowing garden earthworms are built for a completely different life — tunnels through soil, not a crowded bin of banana peels.

Red wigglers: the number one pick

If you buy one species, make it red wigglers. Eisenia fetida is the worm behind the vast majority of home vermicomposting setups, and the reasons are practical.

They are small — usually two to three inches long — and reddish-brown with yellowish banding. They feed in the top few inches of bedding, which is exactly where your food scraps sit. They handle typical indoor temperatures comfortably, roughly 55 to 80°F, so a closet, laundry room, or kitchen corner works fine.

They also reproduce quickly. A healthy bin of red wigglers builds its population on its own within a few months, which means your starter colony grows to match the food you provide. And each worm eats about half its body weight per day, so a pound of red wigglers processes roughly half a pound of scraps daily.

For an apartment bin, red wigglers are the default recommendation for good reason. If you’re comparing them head-to-head with the runner-up, read red wigglers vs European nightcrawlers.

European nightcrawlers: the strong second choice

European nightcrawlers (Eisenia hortensis) are the other true composting species worth knowing. They are bigger — three to five inches — and they burrow deeper into the bedding than red wigglers do.

Their standout trait is temperature tolerance. They handle a wider range than red wigglers, including cooler conditions, which makes them a good fit if your bin lives in a cool basement corner or an unheated mudroom. They reproduce more slowly, so a new bin takes longer to reach full capacity, and they appreciate deeper bedding — twelve inches or more suits them better than a shallow tote.

One naming trap to avoid: “nightcrawlers” sold as fishing bait are often Canadian nightcrawlers (Lumbricus terrestris), which are garden earthworms — not this species. Always check the Latin name when you order.

Why garden earthworms don’t work in bins

It is tempting to dig up a few big earthworms from the yard and drop them in a bin. Don’t. Common garden earthworms are deep burrowers that need vertical soil columns to tunnel through. A shallow bin of food scraps is the opposite of their habitat.

In a bin they tend to do one of two things: try to escape, or slowly die. They eat soil as much as scraps, reproduce slowly, and never settle into the crowded surface-feeding life that composting requires. Save yourself the disappointment and start with a real composting species.

What about African nightcrawlers?

You may see African nightcrawlers (Eudrilus eugeniae) for sale. They are enthusiastic composters and reproduce fast — but they are a tropical species that needs steady warmth year-round. In most homes they are fussier than red wigglers and harder to find from reputable sellers in the US. Unless you live somewhere warm and keep your bin in a consistently heated spot, stick with the first two options.

What does “1 lb of worms” actually mean?

Worms are sold by weight, not by count. One pound of red wigglers is roughly 1,000 worms — a wriggling handful that looks like less than you’d expect. Reputable sellers ship them in moist bedding, and the weight you pay for should be worms, not the bedding they travel in.

For most beginners, half a pound to a full pound is the right starting colony. If you’re unsure how that maps to your kitchen scraps, see how many worms you need for the simple math.

Where to buy composting worms

Buy from a seller that specializes in composting worms, not a bait shop. Look for a live-delivery guarantee, recent reviews from other composters, worms sold by the pound with the species name (Eisenia fetida or Eisenia hortensis) clearly stated, and shipping that avoids extreme heat or cold. If a listing can’t tell you the species, keep looking.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Digging up yard worms. They are the wrong species and won’t survive bin life.
  • Buying bait-shop “nightcrawlers.” These are usually Canadian nightcrawlers — garden earthworms, not composters.
  • Ordering during a heat wave. Worms shipped in extreme temperatures can arrive stressed or dead; check the weather and the seller’s shipping policy.
  • Starting with a thimbleful. A tiny starter colony works, but it will take months to process meaningful scraps. Half a pound is a kinder starting point.

FAQ

Can I use earthworms from my garden for composting? No. Garden earthworms are deep-burrowing species that need soil columns to tunnel through. They don’t thrive in the shallow, crowded, food-rich environment of a worm bin and will try to escape or die off.

How many worms do I need to start? Half a pound to one pound of red wigglers (roughly 500 to 1,000 worms) is the standard starter colony for a household bin.

Do red wigglers smell? No — a healthy worm bin smells like fresh earth. A bad smell means something is off, usually overfeeding. See why your worm bin smells for fixes.

Can I mix red wigglers and European nightcrawlers in one bin? Yes. Some vermicomposters run mixed bins, with red wigglers working the surface and nightcrawlers working the depths. Our red wigglers vs European nightcrawlers comparison covers the trade-offs in detail.
