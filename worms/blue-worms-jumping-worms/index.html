Blue Worms and Jumping Worms for Composting: What to Know

By VermiHome · The Worms · Published November 21, 20268 min read

Blue worms (Perionyx excavatus) are real composting worms — small, fast, and hungry — and they can work in a steadily warm apartment bin. Jumping worms (Amynthas species) are the opposite: invasive pests that should never go into a worm bin, never be bought, and never be released outdoors. If you are choosing worms for an indoor bin, red wigglers are still the safest default; blue worms are a warm-home specialist option, and jumping worms are a hard no.

This guidance is based on widely documented vermicomposting practice, not personal testing.

What are blue worms?

Blue worms, Perionyx excavatus, are a tropical composting species from Southeast Asia. They are smaller than red wigglers — usually 2 to 4 inches long — with a distinctive blue-purple sheen on their skin that gives them their name. They live at the surface of the bedding, like red wigglers, and they eat fast: in steady warmth they can process scraps faster than red wigglers and breed very quickly.

Their catch is temperature. Blue worms want steady warmth, roughly 70 to 85°F, and they sulk or die when temperatures swing or drop. They are also escape-prone: they wander more than red wigglers, especially in a new bin, and they are less forgiving of feeding mistakes, wet bedding, or acidic conditions. A warm, stable apartment is the one indoor setting where they make sense — a cool basement or a drafty balcony bin is not.

For the quick version of how they compare with the other species, see best worms for composting.

What are jumping worms?

Jumping worms are Amynthas species — most commonly Amynthas agrestis and Amynthas gracilis, sometimes sold or found as “Alabama jumpers” or “Georgia jumpers.” They are vigorous, muscular worms, 4 to 8 inches long, that thrash and jump when handled, with a smooth, pale band (clitellum) that wraps all the way around their body, unlike the raised, saddle-shaped band on red wigglers.

They are a problem for three reasons:

  • They are invasive. Extension services in many US states list jumping worms as invasive. They strip the organic layer from soil, leaving behind dry, coffee-ground-like castings that hold little moisture and few nutrients, which damages gardens, lawns, and forests.
  • They reproduce without a mate. A single jumping worm can start a population, so one accidental introduction is enough.
  • They escape. They are strong climbers and thrashers that can get out of bins and containers far more easily than red wigglers.

Jumping worms are not composting worms for home bins. Do not buy them, do not start a bin with them, and never release any bin worms outdoors. If you find worms in your garden that jump and thrash when touched, treat them as a pest problem, not a composting opportunity.

How to tell them apart from red wigglers

Size 3–4 in 2–4 in 4–8 in
Colour Reddish-brown with stripes Blue-purple sheen Grey-brown, glossy
Clitellum (band) Raised, saddle-shaped, does not wrap fully around Raised, reddish Smooth, pale, wraps fully around like a ring
Behaviour when handled Wriggles gently Wriggles, wanders Thrashes, jumps, sheds its tail
Temperature 55–77°F, forgiving 70–85°F, needs steady warmth Broad, but invasive — do not keep

The clitellum is the most reliable field mark: on a jumping worm it is a smooth, light-coloured ring that goes all the way around the body. On red wigglers and blue worms it is raised and does not encircle the body completely. If you are unsure what you have, do not add the worms to your bin — ask your local extension service first.

Blue worms in an apartment bin: when they work

Blue worms can work in an apartment bin when all four of these are true:

  1. Your home stays warm year-round, roughly 70 to 80°F, without cold nights or heating shutdowns. A heated apartment with stable indoor temperatures qualifies; a room that drops below 60°F in winter does not.
  2. You are not a first-time worm keeper. Blue worms punish beginner mistakes — overfeeding, soggy bedding, acidic pockets — faster than red wigglers do. If this is your first bin, start with red wigglers and switch later if you want.
  3. Your bin has a tight lid and no gaps. Blue worms wander, especially in the first week. A secure lid, a light left on over the bin for the first few nights, and a layer of dry bedding on top all help. If worms are leaving the bin, see worms escaping your bin.
  4. You can source them from a reputable seller. Blue worms are harder to find from trustworthy US sellers than red wigglers, and some sellers mislabel other species. Buy only from a supplier that names the species in writing — for guidance on vetting sellers, see where to buy red wigglers online.

If any of those four is not true, red wigglers are the better choice. For a direct head-to-head of the two main species, see red wigglers vs European nightcrawlers.

Which worm should you choose?

For almost every apartment, the answer is red wigglers. They tolerate the widest temperature range, forgive the most mistakes, and are the easiest to buy from reputable sellers. European nightcrawlers are the strong second choice if your home runs cool — they handle temperatures down into the 50s better than red wigglers.

Choose blue worms only if your home is steadily warm, you have kept worms before, and you specifically want their speed and fast breeding. Even then, keep a red wiggler bin as your main system and treat the blue worm bin as an experiment, not your only bin.

Never choose jumping worms. There is no apartment setup where they are a good idea: the escape risk, the invasive status, and the damage they do to soil make them a pest to report and contain, not a pet to keep. If you bought worms and later suspect they are jumpers, isolate the bin, do not release anything outdoors, and contact your local extension service for disposal guidance.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying “jumpers” as composting worms. Some sellers market jumping worms for composting or fishing. The name on the listing is the warning — do not buy them.
  • Mixing species in one bin. Blue worms and red wigglers can share a bin, but the faster species dominates and the mix complicates feeding and harvesting. One species per bin is simpler.
  • Putting a blue worm bin somewhere cold. A balcony in winter, an unheated closet, or a room that drops below 60°F will kill or stall blue worms. Check worm bin winter care before the cold season.
  • Releasing any bin worms outside. Bin worms are not native everywhere, and jumping worms are actively harmful. Finished castings are fine for plants; live worms stay in the bin.

FAQ

Can blue worms and red wigglers live together? Yes, they can share a bin, and in a warm bin the blue worms will usually out-breed the red wigglers. It works, but one species per bin is easier to manage, feed, and harvest.

Are blue worms better composters than red wigglers? In steady warmth, blue worms eat and breed faster. In a normal apartment with temperature swings, drafts, and beginner mistakes, red wigglers are better because they are far more forgiving.

How do I know if worms in my garden are jumping worms? Touch one gently: a jumping worm thrashes violently, jumps, and may drop its tail, and its clitellum is a smooth pale ring around the whole body. Red wigglers and garden earthworms wriggle calmly and have a raised, saddle-shaped band.

I think my bin has jumping worms. What should I do? Do not release anything outdoors. Keep the bin sealed, stop adding food, and contact your local extension service for identification and disposal advice. Do not move the bin’s contents to the garden or a compost heap.

Can I use jumping worms for fishing? Do not. Using them as bait is one of the main ways they spread to new areas. Use red wigglers or European nightcrawlers for fishing instead.
