Best Bedding for Worm Bins: Coconut Coir vs Shredded Paper vs Peat Moss (2026)

The best bedding for most apartment worm bins is Plantonix Coco Bliss coconut coir, 10 lb, at about $28.99 one-time ($24.64 on Subscribe & Save). One 10 lb brick expands to a large volume of fluffy, pH-neutral bedding that holds moisture evenly, and it is OMRI-listed for organic use. If you want to spend nothing, shredded newspaper and cardboard — free from your recycling — work perfectly well as bedding, and this guide explains when free beats bought.

Bedding is not decoration. It is the worms’ home: it holds the moisture they breathe through, it provides the carbon that balances nitrogen-rich food scraps, and it is where they retreat when the bin gets too wet, too dry, or too acidic. Get bedding wrong and you get smell, escape attempts, and dead worms. Get it right and the bin mostly runs itself. The three classic beddings are coconut coir, shredded paper or cardboard, and peat moss — and each wins in a different situation.

1. Plantonix Coco Bliss, 10 lb — best overall

Coco Bliss is a compressed brick of coconut coir — the fibre from coconut husks — that expands to many gallons of loose bedding when you soak it in water. The 10 lb brick lists at $28.99 one-time on Amazon, or $24.64 with Subscribe & Save, and a 5-pack of 650 g bricks lists at a typical price of $24.99. It is OMRI-listed, pH-balanced, and low in salts, which matters because salty coir can harm worms; cheap unwashed coir is the main reason coir gets a bad name.

Coir’s strength is consistency. It holds up to eight times its weight in water and releases it slowly, so the bin stays at the damp-sponge moisture worms need without swinging wet and dry. It also breaks down slowly, so bedding lasts months before it needs topping up. The weakness is price and preparation: you must soak the brick, squeeze out the excess water, and fluff it before use, and $28.99 is real money for what is, after all, coconut husk.

Best for: most apartment bins. If you buy one bedding, buy this one.

2. Urban Worm Coco Coir, 1.4 lb brick — best worm-specific bedding

Urban Worm sells coir specifically as worm bedding, and the 1.4 lb brick costs $14.99 from worms.com, with an 11 lb brick at $30.99. Each 1.4 lb brick absorbs about a gallon of water and expands to over 2.5 gallons of loose bedding. It is the same material as Coco Bliss, sold by a worm company rather than a general garden brand, and the listing describes it explicitly as bedding for worms and reptiles.

The 1.4 lb size is the right amount for one apartment bin: soak one brick, fill the bin three-quarters full, add worms, and you are done. The 11 lb brick at $30.99 is far better value per pound if you run several bins or re-bed often, but it is overkill for a single bin. Per pound, Urban Worm’s small brick is expensive — about $10.70 per pound against $2.90 per pound for Coco Bliss 10 lb — so buy it for convenience and worm-specific sizing, not for value.

Best for: first-time bin setups where you want exactly one bin’s worth of bedding, pre-measured.

3. Miracle-Gro Sphagnum Peat Moss, 1 cu ft — best peat moss

Peat moss is the old-school worm bedding: soft, acidic, and superb at holding moisture. A 1 cubic foot bag of Miracle-Gro Sphagnum Peat Moss costs about $9.98 at Home Depot — a store price tag photographed in April 2026 showed $9.97 — and a 3 lb bag lists at $11.99 from BJ’s Wholesale Club. That makes peat the cheapest bought bedding in this guide by a wide margin: under $10 fills a bin.

Peat’s acidity is both its strength and its weakness. It resists mould and holds moisture well, but a bin bedded entirely in peat can drift too acidic for worms, so you should mix in crushed eggshell or agricultural lime to buffer the pH, and mix peat with shredded cardboard rather than using it alone. Peat is also the least sustainable option here: it is harvested from bogs that take centuries to regrow, which is why many vermicomposters have switched to coir. If sustainability matters to you, choose coir. If price matters most, peat is hard to beat.

Best for: buyers who want the cheapest bought bedding and are willing to buffer pH with eggshell.

4. Shredded paper and cardboard — best free bedding

Shredded newspaper, office paper, and corrugated cardboard cost nothing and work well. Shred them into strips, soak them, squeeze them to damp-sponge wetness, and fluff them into the bin. Cardboard is better than newspaper: it holds its structure longer, keeps air pockets open, and breaks down more slowly. Avoid glossy paper, coloured ink-heavy flyers, and tape — plain black-and-white newsprint and unprinted cardboard are safest.

Free bedding has two honest drawbacks. First, it dries out faster than coir and compacts sooner, so you need to top it up more often. Second, it takes preparation time: shredding a bin’s worth of cardboard by hand is a real chore, and a paper shredder helps enormously. But for an apartment bin, where the bin is small and the cardboard box from your last delivery is already in the recycling, free bedding is the rational default. Many experienced worm keepers use shredded cardboard as their main bedding and buy coir only for starting a new bin.

Best for: everyone on day one. Start with cardboard, and buy coir only if you want more stable moisture.

5. Ready-mix worm bedding, 1 lb — best no-prep option

If you want bedding with zero preparation, ready-mix bedding is a pre-blended mix of sphagnum peat moss, coco coir, and calcium carbonate, sold as a 1 lb bag at $22.99 in a November 2026 listing roundup. You pour it in, add water, and add worms. The calcium carbonate buffers pH, so you do not need to add eggshell separately, and the blend of coir and peat gives you the moisture holding of both.

The price is the problem: $22.99 per pound is by far the most expensive bedding per volume in this guide, and 1 lb is only enough for a small bin. It makes sense in exactly one situation — you are setting up your first bin today, you have no cardboard, no coir, and no time, and you want the setup to work the first time. For everyone else, a coir brick or free cardboard is better value.

Best for: first-time setups where you want guaranteed-ready bedding with no soaking, shredding, or pH guesswork.

Coconut coir vs shredded paper vs peat moss

The head-to-head comes down to three things: moisture, longevity, and cost.

Moisture: coir wins. It holds water evenly and releases it slowly, so the bin stays damp for weeks. Peat is close behind. Shredded paper dries out fastest and needs more frequent misting.

Longevity: coir wins again. It breaks down slowly, so a coir bed lasts months. Cardboard lasts weeks to a couple of months. Peat lasts well but compacts over time and can go acidic.

Cost: shredded paper wins by definition — it is free. Peat is the cheapest bought option at about $10 a bag. Coir costs $15–$29 per brick, but one brick fills several bins’ worth of bedding, so the per-bin cost is only a few dollars.

Sustainability: coir wins. It is a renewable byproduct of coconut farming. Peat is harvested from slow-growing bogs. Paper is recycled, which is good, but its production is energy-intensive.

The practical answer for most apartments is a mix: coir as the base for stable moisture, plus shredded cardboard on top for structure and free carbon. That combination gives you the best of both, and it is what most experienced keepers actually do.

How to prepare bedding

Whatever bedding you choose, prepare it the same way. Soak it in dechlorinated water — leave tap water out overnight, or use rainwater — then squeeze it until it is as damp as a wrung-out sponge: wet enough to feel moist, dry enough that no water drips when you squeeze. Fill the bin three-quarters full with fluffed bedding, add a handful of crushed eggshell for grit and pH, and let the bin sit for a day before adding worms. Never add worms to dry bedding or to soaking-wet bedding; both kill them.

FAQ

How much bedding does one bin need? Fill the bin about three-quarters full with fluffed, damp bedding. One 1.4 lb coir brick or one 1 cu ft bag of peat is about right for a standard apartment bin.

How often should I replace bedding? Top it up every few weeks as it breaks down, and do a full re-bed every 4–6 months, or when you harvest castings. If the bedding smells sour or has gone slimy, re-bed sooner.

Can I use soil or potting mix as bedding? No. Soil is too dense, compacts, and can contain fertilisers or pesticides that harm worms. Use coir, peat, paper, or cardboard instead.

Is peat moss bad for the environment? Peat is harvested from bogs that store carbon and take centuries to regrow, so it has a real environmental cost. Coir is the more sustainable choice if that matters to you.
