Worm Bin Pests: Ants, Mites and Fungus Gnats — How to Get Rid of Them

Ants, soil mites, and fungus gnats are the three pests that show up in indoor worm bins — and all three are fixable without starting over. Each is a symptom of one condition: ants want dry food, mites want damp bedding, gnats want wet, exposed scraps. Fix the condition and the pest leaves.

This guidance is based on widely documented vermicomposting practice, not personal testing. If you’re dealing with fruit flies specifically, see our fruit fly guide — they look similar to gnats but need a different fix.

Identify the pest first

Don’t treat until you know what you’re looking at. A wrong guess wastes a week.

Pest What you see Where they are What they want
Ants A trail of small black or brown ants marching in a line On the bin, the lid, and the floor around it Dry food and sugar — exposed scraps, sweet fruit
Soil mites Tiny white, brown, or red specks crawling on the bedding surface On top of the bedding, around food pockets Damp bedding and decaying matter
Fungus gnats Small dark flies, thinner than fruit flies, hovering in slow clouds Above the bin and near houseplants Wet soil and wet bedding to lay eggs in

If it flies and hovers over the bin, it’s a fly or a gnat. If it crawls in a line, it’s ants. If it’s a speck that doesn’t fly, it’s mites.

Ants: why they come and how to stop them

Ants show up for two reasons: the bedding is too dry, and food is exposed. A dry bin with scraps sitting on the surface is an invitation — ants can smell sweet fruit from across a kitchen.

Fix it in this order:

  1. Bury all food. No scraps on the surface, ever. Dig a pocket, drop the food in, and cover it with 2–3 inches of bedding. This alone stops most ant visits.
  2. Re-wet the bedding. Spray or sprinkle water until it feels like a wrung-out sponge — damp, not dripping. Dry bedding is ant territory. For moisture targets, see too wet or too dry.
  3. Clean the trail. Wipe the bin, lid, and floor with soapy water to erase the scent trail the scouts left — breaking it stops the march.
  4. Set a water moat. Stand the bin in a shallow tray of water with a drop of dish soap. Ants won’t cross it — but check weekly, because a dry moat is no moat.
  5. Skip sweet fruit for two weeks. Bananas, melon, and mango are ant magnets; feed plain vegetable scraps and coffee grounds instead. Our feeding guide lists which foods attract pests.

Never spray insecticide in or near the bin. Anything that kills ants will kill your worms too — and the residue stays in the castings you’ll later put on your plants.

Ants are the one pest that can genuinely threaten a bin if ignored — but a bin with buried food and damp bedding is one they walk past.

Soil mites: mostly harmless, sometimes a warning

Soil mites are tiny white, brown, or red specks that crawl across the bedding. They are the pest most beginners panic about — and the one that matters least.

Most mites are harmless decomposers that eat decaying matter alongside your worms. A few white mites on the surface of a healthy bin — especially a new one — are normal. Leave them alone.

Mites become a problem only when their numbers explode — thousands covering the surface. That means the bin is too wet and too acidic, usually from overfeeding:

  • Dry the cap. Add a thick layer of dry shredded cardboard on top of the bedding and skip feeding for a few days. Mites live in the damp top layer; drying it pushes their numbers down fast.
  • Stop feeding until the last pocket is gone. Overfeeding is what feeds the mite population. For a schedule, see how often to feed.
  • Add crushed eggshells. A sprinkle of finely crushed, rinsed eggshells balances acidity, which mites dislike.

Red mites deserve a closer look: some species are predatory and can bother worms. Treat them the same way — dry cap, less food — and if they persist for two weeks, scoop out the top inch of bedding and replace it with fresh dry bedding.

Never spray mites. The cure is always drier bedding and less food, not chemicals.

Fungus gnats: the dampness pest

Fungus gnats look like fruit flies but are thinner, darker, and drift in slow clouds instead of darting. The difference is what they want: fruit flies want rotting fruit; gnats want wet bedding and damp soil for their eggs. If your bin is wet and your houseplants have gnats too, it’s gnats.

Tell them apart at a glance:

  • Fruit flies are rounder, tan or brown, with red eyes, and they dart around fast. They hover over fruit and food scraps.
  • Fungus gnats are slender, dark gray or black, with long legs, and they drift slowly. They hover over the bedding surface and your plant pots.

The fix is drying, not trapping — but traps help while you dry:

  1. Dry the top layer. Stop watering, add dry shredded cardboard, and leave the lid ajar a few hours a day. Gnats can’t breed in dry bedding.
  2. Hang yellow sticky traps. One or two cards above the bin catch flying adults and break the breeding cycle. They don’t touch the worms or the bedding.
  3. Bury food deeper. Exposed wet scraps are gnat nurseries. Pocket-feed with 2–3 inches of cover, and skip feeding for a week if the bin is very wet.
  4. Check your houseplants. Gnats move between the bin and damp plant soil. Let the top inch of potting soil dry out between waterings, and the gnats lose their second home.

If you have both gnats and fruit flies, treat the flies first with the fruit fly guide — they breed faster and are easier to eliminate — then dry the bin for the gnats.

Prevention checklist

Most pest visits are preventable with four habits:

  • Bury every feeding under 2–3 inches of bedding.
  • Keep bedding damp like a wrung-out sponge — never soggy, never bone dry.
  • Feed only what the worms finish before the next feeding.
  • Keep a dry cardboard cap on top of the bedding at all times.

A bin run this way gets the occasional scout ant or a few mites, and they leave on their own. If pests keep coming back, one of these four habits has slipped. Two more nevers: don’t move the bin outside to air it out — temperature shock kills worms — and don’t stop feeding for more than two weeks; starving the bin starves the worms, not the pests.

FAQ

Are soil mites dangerous to humans? No. They live in damp organic matter, don’t bite, and don’t infest homes — they stay in the bin. Wash your hands after handling bedding, as after any gardening.

Can ants kill my worms? A few scouts won’t, but a large colony can stress worms and steal food — so don’t ignore a steady trail: bury food, wet the bedding, set a moat.

How do I know if it’s fungus gnats or fruit flies? Fruit flies are rounder and tan with red eyes, and they dart. Fungus gnats are thin, dark, and drift slowly. Gnats also show up around your houseplants; fruit flies stick to the bin and the fruit bowl.

How long until the pests are gone? Ants leave within 2–3 days of burying food and setting a moat. Mites drop within a week of drying the cap. Gnats take 2–3 weeks — you have to outlast their egg cycle, with sticky traps plus dry bedding.
