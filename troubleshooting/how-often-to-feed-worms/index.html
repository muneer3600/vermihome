How Often to Feed Worms: A Simple Schedule

Published October 15, 20266 min read

Feed your worms two to three times per week, burying food in different spots each time, and skip a feeding whenever food from last time is still uneaten. As a rule of thumb, worms can eat roughly half their body weight in food per day — so a pound of worms handles about half a pound of scraps daily at full capacity.

How Much Can Worms Actually Eat?

The widely used guideline is that red wigglers eat about half their body weight per day under good conditions. That is a maximum, not a target — a new bin with a small worm population needs far less.

Worms in your bin Rough daily capacity Practical feeding
1/2 lb (starter handful) ~1/4 lb scraps Small handful, 2x per week
1 lb (typical starter order) ~1/2 lb scraps 1-2 cups scraps, 2-3x per week
2 lbs (established bin) ~1 lb scraps 2-3 cups scraps, 2-3x per week

New bins need a settling-in period. For the first two to three weeks, feed lightly — worms are adjusting to their new home and eat less. Ramp up gradually as you see food disappearing. If you are unsure how many worms you actually have, our guide to how many worms you need helps you estimate your population and bin size.

The Pocket Feeding Method

Pocket feeding is the simplest reliable technique for apartment bins:

  1. Pull back the bedding in one corner or along one side of the bin to make a shallow pocket.
  2. Add your food scraps — vegetable peels, fruit ends, coffee grounds, and crushed eggshells are all good choices. See what to feed worms for the full yes-and-no list.
  3. Cover the food completely with several inches of bedding. Buried food breaks down faster and does not attract fruit flies.
  4. Rotate the spot next time. Feed in a different corner or section so the worms work through the whole bin.

Always chop or tear scraps into small pieces. Smaller pieces mean more surface area, which means faster breakdown and happier worms.

How to Tell If You Are Overfeeding

Overfeeding is the number one beginner mistake, and it causes most bin problems — bad smells, excess moisture, and fruit flies all trace back to too much food. Check before every feeding:

  • Last feeding’s food is still mostly there. If you can still recognize last week’s scraps, skip this feeding. The worms are telling you they have enough.
  • The bin smells sour or like garbage. Uneaten food rots instead of being processed.
  • Bedding is getting soggy. Food scraps are mostly water; too much food means too much moisture.
  • Mites or flies are multiplying. Pest explosions usually follow overfeeding.

When in doubt, feed less. Worms can go a week or more without new food by eating bedding — but they cannot escape a bin full of rotting scraps.

Signs You Might Be Underfeeding

Underfeeding is rare but possible, especially in established bins with large populations:

  • Food vanishes within a day or two of every feeding.
  • Bedding disappears quickly — hungry worms eat the cardboard and paper bedding too.
  • Worms look thin and the bin’s overall population seems to shrink.

If you see these signs, increase feeding gradually. Add a bit more each time rather than dumping in a huge amount at once.

Do Worms Eat Less in Winter?

Yes. Worms are less active in cooler temperatures, and an apartment bin near a drafty window or on a cold balcony will slow down in winter. If food starts lasting longer between feedings, that is normal — just feed less often and let the bin’s rhythm guide you.

Keep the bin in the 55-77°F (13-25°C) range if you can. A bin tucked in a closet or under the sink stays warmer and more consistent than one on a cold balcony.

What Does “Finished” Food Look Like?

You will know the worms have processed an area when the scraps are unrecognizable — transformed into dark, crumbly material that smells like earth. Some bits like eggshell fragments and the occasional avocado pit take much longer; that is normal and not a sign of a problem. What matters is that the bulk of each feeding disappears within one to two weeks in an established bin.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Feeding on a fixed schedule regardless of what is left. Always look before you feed.
  • Adding huge amounts at once. Big feedings rot before worms can process them. Small, frequent feedings win.
  • Feeding foods worms struggle with. Citrus, onions, and spicy foods in large amounts; meat, dairy, and oils always. These slow the bin down or cause odor.
  • Leaving food exposed on the surface. Always bury it. Surface food dries out, attracts flies, and breaks down slowly.

FAQ

Can I feed worms every day? You can, in tiny amounts, but it is unnecessary and risky for beginners. Two to three feedings per week is easier to manage and gives you a clear chance to check whether the previous food is gone.

How long can worms go without food? Healthy worms can survive a week or two without fresh scraps by eating bedding. If you go on vacation, add extra damp shredded cardboard before you leave and they will be fine.

Should I feed more when I first set up the bin? No — feed less. New worms eat lightly for the first few weeks while they settle in. Start with small amounts twice a week and increase only when food consistently disappears.

Do I need to remove uneaten food? If a feeding is still sitting there after two weeks and smells bad, remove the worst of it, add fresh dry bedding, and wait a few days before feeding again. One bad feeding will not ruin a bin if you correct course.
