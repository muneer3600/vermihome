Worm Bin Winter Care for Apartments: Cold-Weather Guide (2026)

Winter is the season that kills worm bins. Red wigglers work best between 55°F and 77°F, slow down sharply below 55°F, and die if the bin freezes. In an apartment, winter care is mostly about one decision: where the bin lives from December to February. A bin indoors in a warm room needs almost no special care. A bin on a balcony or in an unheated garage needs insulation, less food, and a plan for the coldest nights. This guide covers both.

We have not hands-on tested every setup described here. This guide is based on published vermicomposting guides, university extension advice, and experienced growers. The temperature numbers below are the standard published ranges for red wigglers — if your bin is colder than 55°F, the worms are already slowing down.

At a glance

Temperature What happens What to do
77°F+ (25°C+) Worms active, may overheat in sun Shade the bin, check moisture
55–77°F (13–25°C) Ideal range, normal feeding No changes needed
40–55°F (4–13°C) Worms slow, eat little Feed less, add bedding, insulate
32–40°F (0–4°C) Worms barely move, at risk Move indoors or insulate heavily
Below 32°F (0°C) Bin freezes, worms die Must be indoors or heated

The one rule: do not let the bin freeze

Everything else in this guide is secondary. A frozen worm bin is a dead worm bin. Cocoons can sometimes survive a light freeze, but adult worms cannot, and a colony that loses its adults in January takes months to rebuild — if it rebuilds at all. If your bin is outdoors and the forecast says below freezing overnight, act that day, not the next morning.

For apartment dwellers, the simplest winter plan is to bring the bin inside before the first frost. A worm bin is not smelly when it is healthy, and it takes up about as much space as a laundry basket. Put it in a closet, under a sink, in a laundry room, or in any room that stays above 55°F. If you have a balcony bin, this is the move: carry it in, set it on a tray to catch drips, and keep feeding it lightly through winter.

If the bin must stay outside

Some bins cannot come inside — a large outdoor system, a shared balcony where indoor space is impossible, or a bin that is simply too heavy to move. In that case, insulate it like you mean it. Wrap the bin in old blankets, towels, or foam board, and cover the top with a piece of rigid insulation or a thick layer of dry leaves. The goal is to slow heat loss, not to heat the bin: insulation buys time on cold nights but cannot keep a bin warm through a week of freezing weather on its own.

Other outdoor tricks: move the bin against the warm wall of the building, where it picks up radiated heat, and out of the wind — wind strips heat faster than still cold air. Put it on a piece of foam or wood, not directly on cold concrete, which sucks heat out of the bottom. Add a thick layer of dry bedding (shredded cardboard or straw) on top, 4–6 inches deep; bedding is insulation and the worms will burrow into it. And stop harvesting in late fall: a full bin holds heat better than an empty one.

If nights regularly drop below freezing, insulation alone is not enough. You need a heat source — a seedling heat mat under the bin, a string of outdoor-safe lights inside an insulated enclosure, or simply moving the bin into a garage or stairwell for the coldest weeks. If none of that is possible, accept that an outdoor bin in a cold climate is seasonal: harvest the castings in fall, keep a small indoor backup bin with a handful of worms, and restart outdoors in spring.

Feeding in winter: less is more

Worms eat less in the cold. A bin at 50°F might eat a quarter of what it eats at 70°F. The most common winter mistake is feeding at summer rates: the food sits uneaten, rots, and the bin turns acidic and smelly — and in a cold bin, you will not notice until it is a real problem.

Cut feeding by half when the bin drops below 60°F, and by three-quarters below 50°F. Feed only when the previous feeding is mostly gone. Chop food small so it breaks down faster, and favor soft scraps (banana peels, cooked vegetables, coffee grounds) over hard ones (carrot chunks, citrus). Stop adding food entirely if the bin is below 40°F — the worms are barely eating, and the food will just rot.

Keep adding bedding, though. Dry shredded cardboard or newspaper absorbs excess moisture from rotting food and gives worms somewhere warm to hide. In winter, err on the side of too much bedding rather than too little.

Moisture and air in winter

Cold bins have a moisture problem in both directions. A bin left in the rain gets soaked, and cold, wet bedding goes anaerobic fast. A bin brought into a heated apartment can dry out, because indoor winter air is dry and heating systems pull moisture out of everything. Check moisture weekly in winter — more often than in summer, not less.

Bedding should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If it is dripping wet, add dry bedding and check that the drainage holes are not blocked. If it is dry and crumbly, sprinkle water lightly — a spray bottle works better than a jug in winter, because it is easy to overdo it. Keep the lid on: it holds in both heat and moisture.

Do not seal the bin airtight to keep it warm. Worms still need oxygen, and a sealed bin goes anaerobic. Insulation on the outside, ventilation on the inside — that is the combination.

Signs your bin is too cold

Worms tell you they are cold before they die. Look for worms clustered in a tight ball in the center of the bin — they are huddling for warmth. Look for worms pressed against the lid or the warmest side. Look for uneaten food piling up week after week. Look for bedding that feels cold and damp to the touch. Any of these means the bin needs warmth now: move it, insulate it, or both.

If you find worms that are sluggish and pale, they are stressed but alive — warm the bin slowly (do not put it next to a radiator or pour warm water on it; sudden heat shocks them) and stop feeding until they are active again. If the bin has frozen solid, the adults are almost certainly dead. Do not throw the bin out immediately: leave it somewhere warm for two weeks, keep it moist, and check for tiny baby worms — cocoons sometimes survive and hatch. If nothing appears after two weeks, empty the bin, save the castings, and start fresh in spring.

Spring: waking the bin back up

When temperatures stay above 55°F, the bin wakes up on its own. Start feeding lightly — a small handful — and increase over two weeks as the worms become active. Add fresh bedding, because winter bedding is usually compacted and spent. If you kept a small indoor backup bin, use its worms and castings to reseed the outdoor bin: a scoop of active material restarts the microbial community much faster than sterile bedding alone.

Check for problems that built up over winter: a bin that sat untouched for months may have compacted bedding, a blocked spigot, or a layer of unfinished food at the bottom. Fluff the bedding, clear the drain, and remove anything that smells rotten before you resume normal feeding.

Winter checklist

  • Before the first frost: decide where the bin will spend winter, and move it if needed.
  • Insulate outdoor bins: blankets, foam board, or straw, plus a thick dry bedding layer.
  • Cut feeding in half below 60°F, by three-quarters below 50°F, and stop below 40°F.
  • Check moisture weekly — cold bins get wet outside and dry out inside.
  • Keep the lid on, but never seal the bin airtight.
  • Watch for huddling worms and uneaten food — both mean the bin is too cold.
  • Keep a small indoor backup bin if your main bin must stay outside.

Frequently asked questions

Can I leave my worm bin on the balcony all winter? In mild climates where nights stay above freezing, yes, with insulation. In climates with regular frosts, no — bring it in or accept that the colony may not survive.

Do worms hibernate? Not exactly. Red wigglers slow down in the cold and can survive short cold spells by burrowing deep, but they do not have a true hibernation. Prolonged cold kills them.

Should I feed worms in winter at all? Yes, but much less. A warm indoor bin can be fed almost normally. A cold bin should get a quarter or less of its summer ration, and only when the previous food is gone.

What temperature kills red wigglers? Sustained temperatures below freezing (32°F / 0°C) kill adult worms. Brief dips just below freezing may be survived if the bin is insulated and the core stays above freezing — but do not rely on it.

The short version

Do not let the bin freeze. Bring it indoors before the first frost if you can; if you cannot, insulate it heavily, cut feeding to a quarter, and check moisture weekly. Worms that survive winter in a warm bin come out of spring ready to work — worms that freeze do not come back.
