The best worm tea brewer for most houseplant owners is the Worm Brew D10 Compost Tea Aerator Kit at $84.00. It fits a standard 5-gallon bucket, includes a heavy-duty pump, tubing, and mesh brew bags, and it is the cheapest complete kit from a worm-specific brand. If you want a more complete kit with compost and activators included, the TeaLAB 5-Gallon Complete Kit at $115.00 is the better pick.

First, an honest question: do you need a brewer at all? Worm tea — water brewed with worm castings and air — is a gentle liquid feed for houseplants. But you can also make a simple worm casting soak by stirring castings into water and letting it sit, with no pump and no brewer (see how to use worm castings for houseplants and worm tea vs leachate). A brewer earns its money only if you brew often, you want the aeration that keeps the tea oxygenated and microbe-friendly, and you have enough houseplants to use a 5-gallon batch. If you have five houseplants, a brewer is overkill. If you have fifty, it pays for itself.

1. Worm Brew D10 Compost Tea Aerator Kit — best overall

The Worm Brew D10 kit is a 5-gallon bucket aerator system from Bag-A-Nut, sold on the maker’s own site at $84.00. It includes a heavy-duty air pump, vinyl tubing, mesh brew bags, and a bungee cord — everything except the bucket and the castings. The aerator alone (without the pump) costs $29.00 from the same store, which tells you the pump and accessories are about $55 of the kit price.

At $84, it is the cheapest complete brewer in this guide from a brand that specialises in worm products rather than general garden gear. A 5-gallon batch is a lot of tea for houseplants — enough to water dozens of plants — so brew it when you have a watering day planned, and use the tea within a day or two, since aerated tea does not keep.

Best for: most houseplant owners. If you buy one brewer, buy this one.

2. TeaLAB 5-Gallon Complete Compost Tea Brewer Kit — best complete kit

The TeaLAB kit costs $115.00 in a November 2026 roundup, and it is the most complete kit in this guide: it includes the bucket, a BubbleSnake aerator, a brew bag, a commercial air pump with hoses and fittings, plus compost, fish-ball activators, insect frass, humic acid and kelp extract, and a chlorine test kit. In other words, it includes the ingredients as well as the equipment, so you can brew on day one without buying castings separately.

The trade-off is price and noise. Other sellers list the same kit at $169, so $115 is the low end of its range — check the seller before you order. The pump is rated at 714–715 GPH, which is powerful, and one roundup notes it is louder than cheaper pumps, so brew outdoors or in a garage rather than in a living room. For apartment use, that noise matters: a loud pump running for 24 hours in a studio is a real drawback.

Best for: buyers who want everything in one box, including the brew ingredients, and who have somewhere to run a loud pump.

The D9 is Worm Brew’s other 5-gallon kit, at $89.99 from the maker’s site, with the aerator alone at $35.00. There is no published reason to prefer the D9 over the D10 for houseplants; the D10 kit is cheaper and does the same job.

We include it because availability varies: if the D10 kit is out of stock, the D9 is the same brand’s direct substitute at $5.99 more. Do not pay a premium for the D9 when the D10 is available.

Buy KIS if you are a serious grower who brews weekly and cares about microbial counts; otherwise, the Worm Brew D10 does the job.

Best for: serious growers who brew often and want the best-tested aeration design.

Do you need a brewer, or just a bucket?

Before you spend $84–$115, try the free version. Stir a cup of worm castings into a gallon of dechlorinated water, let it sit for a few hours, strain it, and water your plants. That is a casting soak, not aerated tea, but for houseplants the difference is small — and the cost is zero. If you do that for a month and your plants respond well, then a brewer is worth considering for convenience and consistency. If you never get around to the soak, a brewer will sit in a cupboard, and you will have spent $84 on a bucket accessory.

Also note the difference between worm tea and leachate: leachate is the liquid that drains from the bottom of a worm bin, and it is not the same as brewed tea. Leachate can be anaerobic and should be diluted heavily or discarded if it smells bad. Brewed tea is aerated on purpose. Never confuse the two.

How to brew worm tea

The basic method is the same for every brewer in this guide. Fill a 5-gallon bucket with dechlorinated water — leave tap water out overnight, or the chlorine will kill the microbes you are trying to grow. Put a cup or two of worm castings in a mesh brew bag, add a tablespoon of unsulfured molasses as microbe food if your kit recommends it, and run the air pump for 12–24 hours. The tea should smell earthy, not sour. Use it the same day, diluted if your kit says so, and water your plants as normal. Clean the brewer after every batch — a dirty brewer grows the wrong microbes next time.

FAQ

How long does brewed worm tea last? Use it within 24–48 hours. Aerated tea loses its microbial activity quickly once the pump stops, and it can go anaerobic if left standing.

Can I brew worm tea without a pump? Yes — that is a casting soak, and it works for houseplants. A pump adds oxygen, which supports more microbes, but the soak is a reasonable free alternative.

How often should I feed houseplants with worm tea? Every two to four weeks during the growing season is plenty. Worm tea is a gentle feed, not a fertiliser replacement, so do not use it every watering.

Is worm tea the same as the liquid from my worm bin? No. Bin liquid is leachate, which can be anaerobic. Brewed worm tea is aerated on purpose. Treat them as different products.
