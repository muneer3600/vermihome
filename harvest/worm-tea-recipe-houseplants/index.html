Worm Tea Recipe: How to Brew Worm Tea for Houseplants (2026)

By VermiHome · Harvest & Use · Published November 7, 20268 min read

The best worm tea recipe for houseplants is simple: 1 gallon of dechlorinated water, 1/4 lb (about 1/2 cup) of finished worm castings, and 1/2 tablespoon of unsulfured molasses, brewed with an aquarium air pump for 24 hours. Use it the same day as a soil drench — about 1/2 cup per 6-inch pot, once a month. That is the whole recipe. The rest of this guide is about getting the details right, because the two mistakes that ruin worm tea are chlorine in the water and brewing without air.

Worm tea is not fertilizer in the usual sense. Finished worm castings have a mild nutrient profile (roughly 1-0.5-0.5 NPK), and the tea is weaker still. What worm tea really delivers is microbes: the brewed liquid carries the bacteria and fungi from your castings into the root zone, where they help roots take up nutrients. Think of it as a microbial inoculant with a little nutrition, not a replacement for feeding your plants.

We have not hands-on tested every brewing method described here. This guide is based on published vermicomposting guides, grower recipes, and extension advice. No product prices are listed because the recipe needs only things you probably own: a bucket, a mesh bag, and a small aquarium pump.

What you need

You need a clean food-grade bucket (1 or 5 gallons), finished worm castings, unsulfured molasses, and a mesh bag — an old clean cotton sock works. For the aerated recipe, you also need a small aquarium air pump with tubing and an air stone. The pump is the one thing worth buying if you do not own one: it is what keeps the brew aerobic, and aerobic is the whole point. A still, unaerated bucket of castings and molasses can go anaerobic, and anaerobic tea smells rotten and can harm roots.

Water matters more than anything else. Tap water contains chlorine, and chlorine kills the microbes you are trying to grow. Dechlorinate by letting tap water sit uncovered for 24 hours so the chlorine off-gasses, or run the air pump in the plain water for 12 hours before adding castings. Rainwater or filtered water works as-is. Do not use softened water — the salt is bad for plants.

The recipe, step by step

Step 1: Dechlorinate the water. Fill your bucket and let it sit uncovered for 24 hours, or aerate it for 12 hours. If you are in a hurry, filtered or rainwater skips this step entirely.

Step 2: Bag the castings. Put 1/4 lb of finished castings (about 1/2 cup) per gallon into the mesh bag or sock and knot it closed. The bag keeps grit out of your watering can and sprayer. Only use finished castings — dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling material with no recognizable food in it. Unfinished material makes weak, smelly tea.

Step 3: Add molasses. Stir 1/2 tablespoon of unsulfured molasses per gallon into the water until it dissolves. Molasses is food for the microbes; it is what makes their population multiply during the brew. Use unsulfured molasses only — sulfured molasses can harm microbes. For a 5-gallon batch, use 1 tablespoon total.

Step 4: Aerate for 24 hours. Submerge the bag and the air stone, turn on the pump, and let it bubble vigorously for 24 hours. You want strong bubbling through the whole bucket, not a gentle fizz. Brew at room temperature, roughly 65–75°F. Cooler water slows the brew; warmer water can cook it. Microbe populations peak around 18–24 hours, then collapse as the molasses runs out — so do not brew for 48 hours thinking longer is better.

Step 5: Use it the same day. Turn off the pump, remove the bag (toss the spent castings on a garden bed or back into the bin), and use the tea within 4–6 hours. Once aeration stops, the aerobic microbes start dying off fast. Do not bottle it and save it for next week.

How to use worm tea on houseplants

For houseplants, the soil drench is the right method: pour the tea at the base of the plant until it saturates the root ball. About 1/2 cup per 6-inch pot, once a month, is plenty. Larger plants take more — a gallon pot can take a full cup or two. For a foliar spray, strain the tea through a fine cloth into a spray bottle and mist the leaves in the morning or evening, never in direct sun.

Dilution is forgiving for established houseplants: full-strength aerated tea is fine as a drench. For seedlings and very young transplants, dilute 1 part tea to 10 parts water — full-strength tea can overwhelm tiny root systems. Apply every 2–4 weeks during the growing season; in winter, when growth slows, once a month or not at all is fine.

The quick steep (no air pump)

If you do not own an air pump, you can still make a useful extract: 1–2 tablespoons of castings per gallon of dechlorinated water, stirred every few hours over 24 hours. This is a steep, not a true aerated brew — the microbe count is much lower, but it still delivers soluble nutrients and some microbes. Use it the same day. Do not add molasses to a non-aerated steep: without air, the sugar feeds the wrong microbes and the bucket can turn anaerobic and smelly.

Worm tea vs leachate: do not confuse them

Worm tea is made on purpose from finished castings, water, and air. Leachate is the liquid that drains out of the bottom of a worm bin — it is not the same thing. Leachate can contain pathogens and unfinished material, and it should never be used undiluted on leafy greens, root vegetables, or anything eaten raw. If your bin produces leachate, dilute it 1 part to 10 parts water and use it only on ornamental plants, or pour it back through the bin. Real worm tea, brewed with air for 24 hours, is the safe option for houseplants and edibles.

Mistakes that ruin worm tea

Chlorinated water. The most common mistake. If you skip dechlorinating, the chlorine kills the microbes before the brew even starts, and you end up with brown water.

Brewing too long. Past 24–36 hours the molasses is gone, the good microbes die back, and anaerobic microbes take over. The tea starts to smell sour or rotten. If it smells bad, throw it out — do not pour it on your plants.

Brewing in the sun or heat. Sunlight and heat above about 80°F encourage harmful microbes. Brew indoors at room temperature, out of direct sun.

Storing brewed tea. Worm tea is a living culture, not a bottled product. Once the pump is off, the clock is running. Use it within hours, not days.

Using unfinished castings. If your castings still contain recognizable food scraps or smell sour, they are not finished. Tea made from them is weak and can smell bad. Wait until the castings are dark, crumbly, and earthy.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I give my houseplants worm tea? Once every 2–4 weeks in the growing season is enough. More is not better — the microbes need time to establish, and overwatering with tea is still overwatering.

Can I use worm tea on all houseplants? Yes. Monsteras, pothos, fiddle-leaf figs, succulents, herbs — all respond. For succulents and cacti, use it less often (once a month at most) because they prefer dry conditions.

Does worm tea replace fertilizer? No. Worm tea is a microbial boost with mild nutrition. Plants that need real feeding — fast-growing vegetables, heavy feeders — still need a proper fertilizer. Use tea alongside feeding, not instead of it.

My tea smells bad. Is it ruined? Yes. Good worm tea smells earthy, like fresh soil. A sour, rotten, or ammonia smell means it went anaerobic. Throw it out, clean the bucket, and brew again with fresh water and a working air pump.

The short version

One gallon of dechlorinated water, 1/4 lb of finished castings in a mesh bag, 1/2 tablespoon of unsulfured molasses, and an aquarium pump running for 24 hours at room temperature. Use it the same day, half a cup per 6-inch pot, once a month. Skip the chlorine, skip the sun, and never confuse it with bin leachate.
