How Long Does It Take to Make Worm Castings? (2026)

By VermiHome · Harvest & Use · Published November 7, 20268 min read

A first harvest of worm castings takes 3–6 months in most home bins. After that, once the worm colony is established and the bin is running steadily, you can harvest every 2–3 months. The range is wide because the timeline depends on how many worms you started with, how warm the bin is, what you feed, and how big the bin is. A small bin started with 1 lb of worms in a warm room can be ready in 2–3 months; a cold bin started with a handful of worms can take 6 months or more.

That is the honest answer, and it surprises people who expect compost in weeks. Worms are not fast. A red wiggler eats about half its body weight per day, and a new bin needs time for microbes to build up, worms to settle in, and the population to grow. The wait is normal, and the first harvest is always the slowest.

We have not hands-on tested every setup described here. Timings in this guide are based on published vermicomposting guides, university extension advice, and experienced growers. Your bin will vary — use the signs of readiness below, not the calendar alone.

At a glance

Situation First harvest Later harvests
Warm room (65–77°F), 1 lb worms, regular feeding 2–3 months Every 2–3 months
Average home bin, 1/2 lb worms 3–4 months Every 2–3 months
Cold room or balcony, few worms, irregular feeding 5–6 months Every 3–4 months
New bin started with worms in sterile bedding 5–6 months Every 2–3 months once mature

What actually takes the time

Making castings is a two-stage process, and both stages need time. First, microbes colonize the bedding and start breaking down food scraps — this takes weeks in a new bin, because fresh bedding is nearly sterile. Worms eat the microbe-rich material, not raw scraps, so nothing much happens until the microbial community is established. Second, the worms themselves need to multiply. It takes about 60 days for a young worm to mature enough to reproduce, and each cocoon takes 3–4 weeks to hatch. A bin that starts with 500 worms can hold several thousand within a few months — but only if conditions are right.

That is why the first harvest is slow and later harvests are fast. After the first harvest, the bin already has microbes, a large worm population, and partially processed material. The system is running. From then on, the cycle is roughly every 2–3 months: fill a tray or a side with food and bedding, let the worms work through it, harvest, repeat.

The five factors that control speed

1. Number of worms. This is the biggest factor. A bin with 1 lb of worms (about 1,000) processes roughly 1/2 lb of scraps per day. A bin with 1/4 lb processes a quarter of that. If your first harvest is slow, the most likely reason is that you started with too few worms. You can add more worms at any time — it is the fastest way to speed up a slow bin.

2. Temperature. Red wigglers work best between 55°F and 77°F (13–25°C). Below 55°F they slow down noticeably; below 40°F they barely eat. A bin in a cold garage in winter can take twice as long as the same bin in a warm kitchen. Keep the bin in the warmest suitable spot in your home, and in winter insulate it or move it indoors.

3. Moisture. Bedding should feel like a wrung-out sponge — damp, not dripping. Too dry, and worms cannot eat or breathe through their skin; too wet, and the bin goes anaerobic, which slows everything and causes smells. Check moisture weekly and add dry shredded cardboard when it is too wet, or a sprinkle of water when it is too dry.

4. Food. Worms process soft, small food fastest: fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, and crushed eggshells. Large chunks, whole vegetables, and tough material like citrus peels or onion take much longer. Chop or freeze food before feeding — freezing bursts cell walls and makes scraps break down faster. Avoid meat, dairy, oily food, and citrus in large amounts; they slow the bin and attract pests.

5. Bedding and carbon. Worms need carbon-rich bedding (shredded cardboard, newspaper, coconut coir) to balance the nitrogen-rich food scraps. A bin fed only food scraps, with no fresh bedding, turns wet and acidic and slows down. Add a handful of fresh bedding every time you feed, and add a full layer every month or two.

How to speed up your first harvest

If you are impatient — and most beginners are — there are honest ways to shorten the wait. Start with more worms: 1 lb instead of 1/2 lb cuts weeks off the timeline. Start with mature bedding: a scoop of finished compost, garden soil, or castings from an established bin inoculates your new bin with microbes on day one, instead of waiting weeks for them to build up. Some sellers pack worms in active vermicompost for exactly this reason, and growers report harvests in 2.5–3 months with mature bedding versus 5–6 months with sterile bedding.

Keep the bin warm and moist, feed small amounts often instead of large amounts rarely, and chop food finely. Do not overfeed: uneaten food rots, heats up, and can kill worms. A good rule is to feed only when most of the previous feeding is gone.

How to tell castings are ready

Do not harvest by the calendar — harvest by the signs. Finished castings are dark brown to black, crumbly, and uniform, like fine coffee grounds. They smell like damp earth, not like food or rot. You should see almost no recognizable food scraps or bedding in the finished material. If you can still pick out pieces of carrot or newspaper, it is not ready.

The simplest readiness test: stop feeding one side of the bin (or the bottom tray) for two weeks. If the material there turns dark and uniform while the worms migrate toward the fresh food on the other side, it is ready to harvest. In a stacking tray system, the bottom tray is ready when the worms have mostly moved up into the tray above it.

How much do you get?

A home bin does not produce a lot of castings. A rough rule from experienced growers: about 2,000 red wigglers can produce more than 7 lb of castings in a month once the bin is mature — but that is a mature, well-fed bin, not a new one. For a typical apartment bin, expect a few pounds per harvest, every 2–3 months. That sounds small, but castings are potent: a little goes a long way. A handful top-dressed on a houseplant, or a 10–20% mix into potting soil at repotting, is enough. You do not need buckets of the stuff.

What to do while you wait

The first 3 months are the hardest because there is nothing to harvest. Use the time well: learn your bin’s rhythm, keep a simple feeding log, and fix problems early. Fruit flies, smells, or escaping worms in month one are much easier to fix than in month four. Read our troubleshooting guides if anything looks wrong — a healthy bin in month one becomes a productive bin in month three.

And do not harvest early. Pulling unfinished material out of the bin removes worms, cocoons, and microbes the colony still needs. It also gives you poor-quality castings full of recognizable scraps. Wait for the signs. The first harvest is worth waiting for.

Frequently asked questions

Can I harvest castings in the first month? No, not in any meaningful amount. In the first month the worms are still settling in and the bedding is barely processed. There is nothing finished to harvest yet.

Why is my bin taking longer than 6 months? The most common reasons are too few worms, a cold bin, overfeeding, or bedding that is too wet or too dry. Check temperature first, then worm numbers, then moisture. Adding more worms and a scoop of mature compost usually fixes a slow bin.

How often should I harvest after the first time? Every 2–3 months once the bin is established. Harvesting more often than that removes too many worms and cocoons; harvesting less often is fine, the castings just accumulate.

Do worms ever stop making castings? No, as long as they are alive, fed, and warm enough. A bin that seems to have stopped is usually too cold, too dry, too wet, or out of food. Fix the condition and production resumes.

The short version

First harvest: 3–6 months, most often around 3–4 months in a warm home bin. After that, every 2–3 months. Start with enough worms, keep the bin warm and damp, feed small and often, and harvest when the castings are dark, crumbly, and earthy — not by the calendar.
