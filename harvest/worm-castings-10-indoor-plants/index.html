Worm Castings for 10 Indoor Plants: Exact Doses for Each

Every indoor plant benefits from worm castings, but not every plant wants the same dose. Leafy growers like pothos and monstera take the full dose every few months; succulents and snake plants want half as much, far less often; and orchids should skip castings entirely. The rule for all ten plants below: start from the pot-size dosage table — tablespoons per pot size — then apply the per-plant adjustment listed here.

This guidance is based on widely documented vermicomposting practice, not personal testing.

How to read the doses below

Each plant entry gives three things: the dose (as a fraction of the standard pot-size dose), the frequency, and the method — top-dressing (sprinkling castings on the soil surface) or mixing in at repotting. If your plant is in a 6-inch pot, the standard dose is 2 tablespoons; a “half dose” for that pot is 1 tablespoon. For the full pot-size table and the 1:4 repotting mix ratio, see best worm castings for houseplants. For how to apply castings in general, see how to use worm castings for houseplants.

Pothos

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months, spring through autumn
  • Method: Top-dress, then water in

Pothos is a heavy feeder for a houseplant and the easiest plant to dose: give it the full table dose for its pot size. It grows fast, so it uses the nutrients quickly. In winter, when growth slows, drop to half the dose or skip a round.

Monstera

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months in the growing season
  • Method: Top-dress, or mix in at repotting

Monstera is another hungry leafy grower and takes the full dose happily. A mature monstera in a 10- or 12-inch pot takes the full 4-tablespoon to ⅓-cup dose. Feed it most in spring and summer when it is pushing out new leaves; in winter, halve the dose.

Snake plant

  • Dose: Half the standard dose
  • Frequency: Once or twice a year, spring only
  • Method: Mix in at repotting, not top-dressing

Snake plants evolved for lean, dry soil and need very little feeding. Half the table dose, once in spring and optionally once in early summer, is plenty. Do not top-dress a snake plant heavily — castings hold moisture, and wet soil around a snake plant’s roots invites rot. Mixing a small amount into the soil at repotting is the safer method.

Fiddle-leaf fig

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months, spring through early autumn
  • Method: Top-dress, water in gently

Fiddle-leaf figs are hungry when they are growing and fussy when they are not. Give the full dose while the plant is actively growing new leaves, and stop completely in winter. Water gently after top-dressing — a heavy pour washes the castings off the surface and down the pot.

Peace lily

  • Dose: Full standard dose in the growing season, half in winter
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months, year-round at the reduced winter dose
  • Method: Top-dress

Peace lilies flower, and flowering plants use more nutrients than foliage-only plants during bloom. Give the full dose in spring and summer while it is flowering, then half the dose in autumn and winter.

Spider plant

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months in the growing season
  • Method: Top-dress

Spider plants are fast growers and heavy feeders, and they respond visibly to castings — greener leaves and more plantlets. Give the full table dose for the pot size.

Philodendron

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 2–3 months, spring through autumn
  • Method: Top-dress, or mix in at repotting

Philodendrons behave much like pothos: leafy, fast-growing, and happy with the full dose. Climbing types on a moss pole and trailing types in a hanging basket take the same dose for their pot size. In winter, halve the dose or skip it.

Calathea

  • Dose: Half the standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 3–4 months, spring and summer only
  • Method: Top-dress lightly

Calatheas are sensitive to salts and rich fertilisers, and castings are gentler than chemical fertiliser — but they still want a light hand. Half the table dose, spread thinly, is right. Water with room-temperature water after feeding, and never feed a calathea in winter when it is barely growing.

ZZ plant

  • Dose: Half the standard dose
  • Frequency: Once a year, in spring
  • Method: Mix in at repotting, not top-dressing

The ZZ plant stores water and nutrients in its thick rhizomes and needs almost no feeding. One half-dose in spring — ideally mixed into the soil at repotting — is enough for the whole year. Like the snake plant, it hates wet soil, so do not top-dress it heavily: castings on the surface hold moisture against the stems. If your ZZ plant is growing slowly, that is normal; do not try to force it with extra castings.

Windowsill herbs (basil, mint, parsley)

  • Dose: Full standard dose
  • Frequency: Every 4–6 weeks while harvesting
  • Method: Top-dress, then water in

Herbs are the exception to the every-few-months rule: you are constantly cutting them, so they are constantly regrowing, and they use nutrients fast. Give the full table dose for the pot size every 4–6 weeks while you are harvesting regularly. Mint is the hungriest of the three and will take the full dose without complaint.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Feeding every plant the same dose. Snake plants and ZZ plants want half the dose, once a year; pothos and monstera want the full dose every few months. The plant’s growth rate sets the dose, not the pot alone.
  • Top-dressing succulents and ZZ plants heavily. Castings hold moisture. On dry-soil plants, mix a small amount into the soil at repotting instead of piling it on the surface.
  • Feeding a dormant plant. A plant that is not growing cannot use the nutrients. Winter feeding at full strength pushes soft growth that the plant cannot support.
  • Using castings as a cure for every problem. Yellow leaves, drooping, or brown edges are usually light, water, or humidity problems. Castings fix hunger, not everything else.
  • Applying castings and harvesting herbs the same day. Feed after you cut, and give the plant a few days before the next harvest.

FAQ

Can I use worm castings on all my houseplants? Almost all of them — the ten above cover the common cases. The main exceptions are orchids and carnivorous plants, which should skip castings entirely, and succulents, which want only a half dose mixed into the soil.

How do I know if I gave too much? Overfeeding with castings is hard to do, but the signs are soft, leggy growth, a musty smell from the soil surface, or fungus gnats. If you see those, scrape off the top layer, let the soil dry, and skip the next feeding.

Should I mix castings into the soil or top-dress? Top-dressing is easiest and works for most plants. Mixing in at repotting is better for snake plants, ZZ plants, and succulents, and for any plant you are repotting anyway. Both methods work — pick the one that fits the plant.

Do castings replace liquid fertiliser? For most houseplants, yes — a regular castings schedule covers their needs. Very heavy feeders in peak summer can take a half-strength liquid feed as well, but it is optional, not required.

Can I use castings on seedlings? Yes, but lightly: a thin sprinkle mixed into seed-starting mix, not a full dose. Seedlings need very little, and too much richness can burn their roots.
