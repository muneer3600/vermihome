Worm Tea vs Leachate: What's Safe to Use?

Published October 15, 20266 min read

Leachate is the liquid that drains from the bottom of your worm bin — it’s variable and can be anaerobic, so treat it cautiously: dilute it, keep it off edible plant parts, and never drink it. Aerated worm tea is different: finished castings steeped in water with an aquarium air pump and a microbial food source, brewed about 24 hours and used fresh. The internet constantly confuses the two. They are not the same thing.

What Is Leachate?

Leachate (sometimes called “worm bin drainage,” or misleadingly labeled “worm tea” on products) is whatever liquid drips down through your bin and collects at the bottom. It can come from wet food scraps, condensation, or excess moisture in the bedding.

Here’s the problem: you don’t know what’s in it. In a healthy, well-aerated bin it’s mostly excess water with some dissolved nutrients. In a bin that’s gone soggy or anaerobic, that liquid can harbor the wrong microbes and smell foul. Because leachate’s history is unknown — especially in a new or troubled bin — the safe approach is caution.

Cautious rules for leachate

  • Dilute it — roughly 10 parts water to 1 part leachate is a common cautious ratio.
  • Use it on ornamental houseplants and outdoor ornamentals only, not on leafy greens or herbs you’ll eat raw.
  • Skip it entirely if it smells rotten or foul — that signals anaerobic conditions. Pour it down the drain and fix the moisture problem in your bin instead (see worm bin too wet or dry).
  • Never drink it. This should go without saying, but leachate is not a health tonic no matter what a forum post claims.
  • Don’t store it — use it or discard it the same day.

If your bin produces a lot of leachate, that’s feedback: you’re likely overfeeding wet foods or the bedding needs more dry carbon. A well-run bin produces very little drainage.

What Is Aerated Worm Tea?

Actively aerated worm tea (often just “worm tea” or “compost tea”) is a deliberate brew: finished worm castings suspended in dechlorinated water, aerated with an aquarium pump for around 24 hours, usually with a small amount of microbial food like unsulfured molasses. The goal is to multiply the beneficial microbes from the castings into a liquid you can water or spray onto plants.

The key word is aerated. Continuous bubbling keeps oxygen levels high, which favors beneficial aerobic microbes and discourages anaerobic ones. That’s the fundamental difference from leachate, which just sat at the bottom of a bin.

Note: “worm tea” is also sometimes used to mean simply castings soaked in water without aeration (a “castings extract”). That’s milder and simpler — fine as a gentle liquid feed — but it’s not the aerated brew described here.

How to Brew Basic Aerated Worm Tea (Safely)

You need: a bucket, an aquarium air pump with an air stone, finished worm castings, and unsulfured molasses.

  1. Dechlorinate the water. Fill a 5-gallon bucket and let it sit uncovered for 24 hours, or run the air pump in it for an hour — chlorine can harm the microbes you’re trying to grow. If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, a full day of sitting is the safer bet.
  2. Add castings. About 2–4 cups of finished castings per 5 gallons of water. Put them in a mesh bag, old pillowcase, or loose — a bag makes straining easier. Need castings first? See how to harvest worm castings.
  3. Add food. A tablespoon or two of unsulfured molasses per 5 gallons feeds the microbes during brewing. This is the standard starting point in most compost-tea recipes.
  4. Aerate for 24 hours. Drop in the air stone, keep the pump running the whole time, and keep the bucket at room temperature out of direct sun.
  5. Use it fresh. Strain and apply within a few hours of finishing the brew — ideally the same day. Without aeration, the microbial population declines quickly and the tea can turn anaerobic.

How to Use Worm Tea

  • Soil drench beats foliar spray for beginners. Pouring it on the soil is simple and low-risk. Foliar spraying works too, but do it in the evening to avoid leaf burn in sun, and strain well so you don’t clog the sprayer.
  • Dilute or use straight? Opinions vary; a common approach is using it undiluted as a soil drench, or diluting roughly 1:1 with water to stretch it. Either way, apply to moist soil, not bone-dry pots.
  • Use it the day it’s brewed. This is the single most repeated rule in tea brewing, and it’s worth repeating.
  • Don’t brew longer than about 36 hours. Past that, the food runs out, microbes crash, and the brew can go anaerobic — you’ll smell it turn.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Calling leachate “worm tea.” They’re different liquids with different risk profiles. Labels and forum posts blur them constantly — now you know better.
  • Brewing without aeration and expecting the same result. A bucket of castings sitting in still water is an extract, not aerated tea. It’s fine as a mild feed, just don’t expect the microbial bloom.
  • Using foul-smelling tea. If your brew smells rotten, sour, or putrid instead of earthy, discard it and start over — something went anaerobic.
  • Storing brewed tea for days. It’s a living culture, not a bottled product. Brew it, use it, done.
  • Applying leachate to edible crops. Keep drainage liquid away from anything you’ll eat, especially raw.

FAQ

Is the liquid from my worm bin the same as worm tea? No. Bin drainage (leachate) is incidental runoff of unknown quality. Real worm tea is an intentional aerated brew made from finished castings. Treat them very differently.

Can I drink worm tea or leachate? No. Neither is for human consumption, ever.

How long does brewed worm tea last? Use it within hours — the same day you brew it. Once aeration stops, beneficial microbes decline and the tea can turn anaerobic.

Do I need the molasses? It’s the standard microbial food in most recipes and helps the brew along. If you’d rather skip it, you’ll get a milder castings extract — still useful, just less of a microbial bloom.
