The best compost bin for most apartments is a worm bin — specifically the VermiHut Plus 5-Tray at $69–$82 — because it is the only apartment system that turns kitchen scraps into finished, ready-to-use compost indoors. If you only need somewhere to collect scraps before they go to a drop-off or an outdoor bin, a $30 countertop bin is the better buy. And if you want a machine that does the work for you, an electric composter costs $499–$599 and still does not make real compost.

“Compost bin” means three different things, and most buying guides mix them up. This guide separates them: full composting systems that make finished compost, countertop collectors that only hold scraps, and electric machines that dry and grind scraps. Pick the type first, then the product.

Full systems: worm bins

A worm bin is the only apartment system that produces finished compost indoors. Red wiggler worms eat your fruit and vegetable scraps and turn them into castings you can use on houseplants the same day you harvest them.

The VermiHut Plus 5-Tray ($69–$82) is the best value: five trays, vertical footprint, and room for beginner mistakes. The Worm Factory 360 ($129.95–$134.95) is the most reviewed bin with a five-year warranty. The Urban Worm Bag V2 ($139–$139.99) has the easiest harvest — open the bottom flap and scrape out castings. All three are covered in detail in best worm bin for apartments and in our umbrella worm bin buying guide.

A worm bin suits you if you mostly produce fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and paper, and you have houseplants to feed. It does not suit you if you mostly throw away meat, dairy, and cooked leftovers — worms cannot handle those. For that waste stream, bokashi is the better system (below).

Full systems: bokashi buckets

A bokashi bucket ferments food waste in a sealed bucket using bran inoculated with microbes. It handles everything a worm bin cannot: meat, dairy, cooked food, and oily scraps. The Sunwood Life Bokashi Compost Kit is the most-cited apartment kit — a 5-gallon bucket with a strainer, spigot, and a 3/4-gallon kitchen pail included.

We could not verify a current price for the Sunwood Life kit on 6 November 2026 — Amazon showed no featured offers for it. Comparable kits are priced at $42.70 (EarthEasy dual-bucket starter kit) and $45.00 (EarthEasy 4.4-gallon single bin), while the premium Bokashi Living 2-bin kit costs $149.50. Check the listing on the day you buy; do not trust an old roundup price.

The catch with bokashi is the output. Fermented scraps are pre-compost, not finished compost — they still need to be buried in soil, added to an outdoor compost system, or handed to a collection service before they are safe to plant into. If you have no balcony soil, no garden, and no drop-off service, bokashi leaves you with a bucket of fermented scraps and nowhere to put them. That is the single most important thing to check before you buy one. The full comparison is in bokashi vs worm composting.

Scrap collectors: countertop bins

A countertop bin does not compost anything. It is a small, lidded bin that sits next to your cutting board and holds scraps for a few days until you empty it into a worm bin, a bokashi bucket, an outdoor bin, or a drop-off. Most apartment composters need one of these as well as a full system — it is the daily habit that makes the system work.

The Epica Countertop Compost Bin (1.3 gallons, about $29.98) is the best overall: stainless steel, charcoal filter, and over 20,000 ratings. The OXO Good Grips Easy-Clean Compost Bin (1.75 gallons, about $29–$31) is the easiest to clean, with a flip-top lid you can open one-handed. Both are covered in detail in best countertop compost bin for apartment.

Do not buy a countertop bin expecting it to make compost. Buy it to make composting convenient.

Electric machines: fast, expensive, and not compost

Electric composters — the Lomi 3 ($549), Vitamix FoodCycler Eco 3 ($499), and Reencle ($499) — heat, dry, and grind scraps in a few hours. The output looks like dry crumbs and is often called compost, but it is not: the heat kills the microbes, and the crumbs still need outdoor composting before they are safe to use on plants. On top of the purchase price, Lomi charges about $175 a year for filters and pods, which pushes its five-year cost to roughly $1,375.

An electric machine suits you if you want zero smell, zero bugs, and zero maintenance, and you have $500 to spend on convenience. It does not suit you if you want finished compost for houseplants — a $70 worm bin does that better. And it does not suit a tight budget: a worm bin plus a countertop bin costs under $110 together.

Which type should you choose?

  • Mostly fruit and veg scraps + houseplants: a worm bin. Start with how to choose a worm bin.
  • Lots of meat, dairy, and cooked leftovers: a bokashi bucket — but only if you have soil, a garden, or a drop-off for the fermented output.
  • You already compost outdoors or use a drop-off: a countertop bin is all you need.
  • You want zero effort and have $500: an electric machine, knowing the output is pre-compost, not compost.
  • You rent and your landlord is strict: a sealed bokashi bucket or a worm bin in a tray — both stay out of sight and low-smell. Check your lease first; some buildings ban composting outright.

What to check before you buy

  • Where does the output go? Worm castings go on houseplants. Bokashi output needs soil. Countertop scraps need a system or a drop-off. Electric crumbs need outdoor composting. If you cannot answer this, do not buy yet.
  • How much space do you really have? Measure the spot — under the sink, in a closet, on the counter — before you order. A tray stack is vertical; a bokashi bucket is a 5-gallon cylinder; an electric machine is the size of a bread machine.
  • Smell control. A charcoal filter (countertop bins), a tight lid (worm bins), or a sealed bucket (bokashi) are non-negotiable in an apartment. If a product has none of these, skip it.
  • Ongoing costs. Worm bins need worms ($38–$65 per lb) and bedding (~$10). Bokashi needs bran refills. Electric machines need filters and pods ($47–$281 a year). Countertop bins need charcoal filter replacements every six months.

FAQ

Can I compost in an apartment with no balcony? Yes. A worm bin under the sink or in a closet works with no outdoor space at all — the castings go on houseplants. A bokashi bucket also works indoors, but you still need somewhere for the fermented output to finish.

Will a compost bin smell up my apartment? It should not. A healthy worm bin smells like fresh earth, a sealed bokashi bucket smells faintly sour only when opened, and a countertop bin with a charcoal filter holds smells for two to three days. A bad smell means something is wrong — usually overfeeding, too much water, or food left exposed. See why your worm bin smells.

What is the cheapest way to start composting in an apartment? A DIY worm bin tote for under $40 — see DIY worm bin — plus a $27–$30 countertop bin for daily scraps. Total under $70, and the only ongoing cost is worms.

Do I need a worm bin and a bokashi bucket? Some households run both: the worm bin for fruit and vegetable scraps, the bokashi bucket for meat, dairy, and cooked food. Start with one — the one that matches most of your waste — and add the second only if the first leaves a waste stream unhandled.
