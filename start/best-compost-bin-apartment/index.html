The best compost bin for most apartments is a worm bin — specifically the VermiHut Plus 5-Tray at $69 — because it is the only apartment system that turns kitchen scraps into finished, ready-to-use compost indoors. If you only need somewhere to collect scraps before they go to a drop-off or an outdoor bin, a $30 countertop bin is the better buy. And if you want a machine that does the work for you, an electric composter costs $360–$549 and still does not make real compost.

“Compost bin” means three different things, and most buying guides mix them up. This guide separates them: full composting systems that make finished compost, countertop collectors that only hold scraps, and electric machines that dry and grind scraps. Pick the type first, then the product.

Full systems: worm bins

A worm bin is the only apartment system that produces finished compost indoors. Red wiggler worms eat your fruit and vegetable scraps and turn them into castings you can use on houseplants the same day you harvest them.

VermiHut Plus 5-Tray — best value ($69)

Five deep trays, 50 litres of capacity, and a 17 × 17 inch footprint: the VermiHut is the cheapest way to get a full-size tray system. It comes with a spigot base, a worm-saver tray, a coconut mat and a V-board for airflow.

  • Best for: households of three or more that cook daily — the most capacity per dollar here.
  • Real-world limitation: five full trays make a tall, heavy stack (33 inches high), and lifting a finished bottom tray takes two hands and clear floor space. Worms are not included, and some owners report worms escaping or getting trapped under the straw mat.
  • Who should buy: anyone who wants maximum volume for minimum money and has a closet or corner for a tall stack.

Worm Factory 360 — most reviewed ($120–$135)

Four trays (expandable to six) on an 18 × 18 inch footprint, with a five-year warranty and the largest owner community of any bin here. Each full tray weighs about 12.5 lb, so lifting is manageable.

  • Best for: first-time worm composters — the most reviewed bin, with the clearest instructions and the most help available online.
  • Real-world limitation: it costs roughly twice the VermiHut for less capacity, and extra trays cost about $25 each. The plastic is not listed as UV-stable, so keep it indoors.
  • Who should buy: beginners who want the proven, best-supported system and are happy to pay for it.

Urban Worm Bag V2 — easiest harvest ($139)

A breathable 900D fabric bag on a metal frame: feed from the top, open the zipper-free bottom and scrape out finished castings. No trays to lift at all, and at 5.3 cu ft it holds the most of any bin here — up to 8 lb of worms processing 3–4 lb of scraps a day.

  • Best for: large households (4–6 people) and anyone who hates lifting trays.
  • Real-world limitation: the 27-inch-square footprint is the biggest here — it needs real floor space, not a closet shelf. The fabric can stain over time.
  • Who should buy: high-scrap households with a utility room, garage or basement corner to spare.

Urbalive — best indoor design (price not verified — check listing)

The only bin here designed by a named industrial designer (Jiri Pelcl, Red Dot Design Award winner), built to sit in a living room like furniture rather than hide in a closet. Two working trays of 21.7 litres each (about 43 litres total), a 15-litre liquid base, wooden legs and a wooden-handled lid, in 100% recycled UV-stabilised plastic. A smaller Compact version (two 15-litre trays in a 38 cm cube) also exists.

  • Best for: apartment dwellers whose bin will sit in a visible room — the only pick here you would not want to hide.
  • Real-world limitation: two trays is a small working volume for a family, and you pay a design premium over plain plastic systems. US pricing was not verifiable at our check — UK complete kits sell for £179.95 with worms included.
  • Who should buy: design-conscious renters and small households (1–2 people) who want a bin that earns its place in the kitchen.

Uncle Jim’s Compact 3-Tray — budget starter (from $74)

Uncle Jim’s Worm Farm has sold worms for over fifty years, and its Compact 3-Tray starter is the smallest store-bought system here: a 9 × 10 inch footprint, 13 inches high with its stand, sold direct from the maker from $74 with free shipping. Worms are sold separately.

  • Best for: singles, couples who eat out often, and anyone testing whether worm composting suits them.
  • Real-world limitation: three small trays fill fast — a household that cooks daily will outgrow it within months. The maker does not publish a capacity figure.
  • Who should buy: first-timers on a tight budget, or a second bin for a classroom or kids’ project.

Hungry Bin — best flow-through, no trays to lift ($359.95)

A single tapered 80-litre (20-gallon) bin on wheels, designed in New Zealand and now made in the USA. Feed from the top; finished castings collect in trays at the bottom that you slide out — no trays to unstack, no digging, no hand-sorting worms. A mature worm population (up to about 6.5 lb of worms) can process up to 4.4 lb of scraps a day.

  • Best for: large households and serious gardeners who compost every day and never want to lift a tray again.
  • Real-world limitation: at $359.95 it costs five times a VermiHut, it is 37 inches tall and needs a dedicated corner, and it was listed as out of stock at the US retailer at our last check. Worms and bedding are not included.
  • Who should buy: households of five or more, or anyone who wants a buy-once, ten-year system and will actually use its capacity daily.

Maze Worm Farm — lowest height (price not verified — check listing)

Two 12-litre trays in a stack only 15 inches high (28 × 38 × 38 cm), made from recycled plastic, with a central drain and a pull-out tray for small harvests. It is the shortest stackable system here — the one that fits under a low shelf or on a balcony table where a tall tray tower will not.

  • Best for: tight spaces — under a low shelf, on a small balcony, or anywhere a 25-inch tower will not fit.
  • Real-world limitation: two small trays are the smallest working volume of any tray system here, and it takes the longest to finish a batch. US pricing was not verifiable at our check — the UK RRP is £79.99.
  • Who should buy: one- or two-person households with very little space who want a real tray system, not a countertop collector.

A worm bin suits you if you mostly produce fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and paper, and you have houseplants to feed. It does not suit you if you mostly throw away meat, dairy, and cooked leftovers — worms cannot handle those. For that waste stream, bokashi is the better system (below).

Full systems: bokashi buckets

A bokashi bucket ferments food waste in a sealed bucket using bran inoculated with microbes. It handles everything a worm bin cannot: meat, dairy, cooked food, and oily scraps. The Sunwood Life Bokashi Compost Kit is the most-cited apartment kit — a 5-gallon bucket with a strainer, spigot, and a 3/4-gallon kitchen pail included.

We could not verify a current price for the Sunwood Life kit on 26 November 2026 — Amazon showed no featured offers for it. Comparable kits are priced at $42.70 (EarthEasy dual-bucket starter kit) and $45.00 (EarthEasy 4.4-gallon single bin), while the premium Bokashi Living 2-bin kit costs $149.50. Check the listing on the day you buy; do not trust an old roundup price.

The catch with bokashi is the output. Fermented scraps are pre-compost, not finished compost — they still need to be buried in soil, added to an outdoor compost system, or handed to a collection service before they are safe to plant into. If you have no balcony soil, no garden, and no drop-off service, bokashi leaves you with a bucket of fermented scraps and nowhere to put them. That is the single most important thing to check before you buy one. The full comparison is in bokashi vs worm composting.

  • Best for: households whose waste is mostly meat, dairy and cooked food — the only system here that takes all of it.
  • Real-world limitation: the output is pre-compost, not finished compost — without soil, a garden or a drop-off, you have nowhere to put it. A single bucket also means no rotation while a batch ferments for two weeks.
  • Who should buy: apartment dwellers with balcony soil, a garden bed or a collection service, who throw away a lot of cooked food.

Scrap collectors: countertop bins

A countertop bin does not compost anything. It is a small, lidded bin that sits next to your cutting board and holds scraps for a few days until you empty it into a worm bin, a bokashi bucket, an outdoor bin, or a drop-off. Most apartment composters need one of these as well as a full system — it is the daily habit that makes the system work.

The Epica Countertop Compost Bin (1.3 gallons, about $30) is the best overall: stainless steel, charcoal filter, and over 20,000 ratings. The OXO Good Grips Easy-Clean Compost Bin (1.75 gallons, about $32) is the easiest to clean, with a flip-top lid you can open one-handed. Both are covered in detail in best countertop compost bin for apartment.

Epica 1.3 gal — best overall (~$30)

  • Best for: durability and odour control — stainless steel, a charcoal filter, and over 20,000 ratings.
  • Real-world limitation: it is not dishwasher-safe (it failed one dishwasher test), and 1.3 gallons fills fast in a household that cooks daily.
  • Who should buy: one- or two-person households that want a steel pail that lasts for years.

OXO Good Grips 1.75 gal — easiest to clean (~$32)

  • Best for: easy cleaning and the largest countertop capacity here (1.75 gallons) — a flip-top lid you can open one-handed, and a smooth interior that rinses clean.
  • Real-world limitation: plastic, not steel, and it has no charcoal filter — odour control relies on airflow through the lid instead.
  • Who should buy: households that cook daily and want the biggest, easiest-to-clean collector on the counter.

Do not buy a countertop bin expecting it to make compost. Buy it to make composting convenient.

Electric machines: fast, expensive, and not compost

Electric composters — the Lomi 3 ($499–$549), Vitamix FoodCycler Eco 3 ($360–$400), and Reencle ($499) — heat, dry, and grind scraps in a few hours. The output looks like dry crumbs and is often called compost, but it is not: the heat kills the microbes, and the crumbs still need outdoor composting before they are safe to use on plants. On top of the purchase price, Lomi charges about $175 a year for filters and pods, which pushes its five-year cost to roughly $1,375.

An electric machine suits you if you want zero smell, zero bugs, and zero maintenance, and you have $500 to spend on convenience. It does not suit you if you want finished compost for houseplants — a $69 worm bin does that better. And it does not suit a tight budget: a worm bin plus a countertop bin costs under $110 together.

Lomi 3 — best grind-and-dry machine ($499–$549)

  • Best for: zero effort and zero smell — load it, press a button, get dry crumbs hours later.
  • Real-world limitation: about $175 a year for filters and pods, which pushes the five-year cost to roughly $1,375, and the output is dry pre-compost, not compost.
  • Who should buy: busy households with $500 to spend on convenience who do not need finished compost.

Vitamix FoodCycler Eco 3 — best value electric ($360–$400)

  • Best for: small batches from a major brand, with the longest standard warranty here (3 years).
  • Real-world limitation: a 2.5-litre bucket means small batches only, and the output still needs outdoor composting before it is safe to use on plants.
  • Who should buy: small households that want the cheapest electric option from a brand they already trust.

Reencle — closest to real compost ($499)

The Reencle is the only electric machine here that composts with live microbes instead of just heating and grinding. It processes about 2.2 lb of scraps a day, runs quietly, and its output is the closest to real compost of any machine here. Buy it outright for $499 or rent for $35 a month.

  • Best for: households that want a machine that actually composts, not one that just dries scraps.
  • Real-world limitation: $499 plus about $47 a year for filters and microbe refills, and the output still benefits from a rest in soil before it goes on plants.
  • Who should buy: anyone who likes the idea of an electric composter but wants a biological process, not a dehydrator.

How to choose: tray, flow-through or in-ground?

Three designs do the same job in different ways.

Stacking trays (upward migration). Trays stack on top of each other. You feed the top tray; when it is full, the worms crawl up through the mesh floor into the next tray of fresh food, leaving finished castings behind in the bottom tray, which you lift out. Simple, cheap, and the most common design — VermiHut, Worm Factory 360, Urbalive, Uncle Jim’s and Maze all work this way. The trade-off is lifting: a full tray weighs 10–13 lb.

Flow-through (continuous flow). One tall bin, no trays. You feed from the top and finished castings fall to the bottom, where you slide them out. Nothing to lift, nothing to unstack — the Hungry Bin and the Urban Worm Bag work this way. The trade-off is size and price: both are bigger and costlier than a tray stack.

In-ground. A perforated container buried in soil or a raised bed. Worms come and go freely, and castings go straight into the soil — there is nothing to harvest. Urbalive makes a 12-litre in-ground unit for beds and borders. This suits balcony gardeners with soil beds, not flats with no outdoor soil at all.

The size rule. Washington State University Extension gives the rule to size by: worms eat about half their body weight in scraps a day, so you need about 2 lb of worms for each pound of food you add per day. A family of four produces about 1 lb of kitchen scraps a day, so it needs about 2 lb of worms — roughly 2,000 red wigglers — and a bin with at least 5 square feet of surface area (Clemson Cooperative Extension).

Household to size, in practice:

Household Scraps per week Worms to start Bin size
1–2 people 3–4 lb 1 lb (about 1,000 worms) 2–3 trays, or a small bin like the Compact 3-Tray or Maze
3–4 people 5–7 lb 2 lb (about 2,000 worms) 4–5 trays (VermiHut, Worm Factory 360)
5+ people 8 lb or more 3 lb or more Flow-through (Hungry Bin, Urban Worm Bag) or two tray bins

Start with fewer worms than the rule suggests and let the population grow — a new colony needs two to three months before it can handle full feeding.

Which type should you choose?

  • Mostly fruit and veg scraps + houseplants: a worm bin. Start with how to choose a worm bin.
  • Lots of meat, dairy, and cooked leftovers: a bokashi bucket — but only if you have soil, a garden, or a drop-off for the fermented output.
  • You already compost outdoors or use a drop-off: a countertop bin is all you need.
  • You want zero effort and have $500: an electric machine, knowing the output is pre-compost, not compost.
  • You rent and your landlord is strict: a sealed bokashi bucket or a worm bin in a tray — both stay out of sight and low-smell. Check your lease first; some buildings ban composting outright.

What to check before you buy

  • Where does the output go? Worm castings go on houseplants. Bokashi output needs soil. Countertop scraps need a system or a drop-off. Electric crumbs need outdoor composting. If you cannot answer this, do not buy yet.
  • How much space do you really have? Measure the spot — under the sink, in a closet, on the counter — before you order. A tray stack is vertical; a bokashi bucket is a 5-gallon cylinder; an electric machine is the size of a bread machine.
  • Smell control. A charcoal filter (countertop bins), a tight lid (worm bins), or a sealed bucket (bokashi) are non-negotiable in an apartment. If a product has none of these, skip it.
  • Ongoing costs. Worm bins need worms ($38–$65 per lb) and bedding (~$10). Bokashi needs bran refills. Electric machines need filters and pods ($47–$281 a year). Countertop bins need charcoal filter replacements every six months.

FAQ

Can I compost in an apartment with no balcony? Yes. A worm bin under the sink or in a closet works with no outdoor space at all — the castings go on houseplants. A bokashi bucket also works indoors, but you still need somewhere for the fermented output to finish.

Will a compost bin smell up my apartment? It should not. A healthy worm bin smells like fresh earth, a sealed bokashi bucket smells faintly sour only when opened, and a countertop bin with a charcoal filter holds smells for two to three days. A bad smell means something is wrong — usually overfeeding, too much water, or food left exposed. See why your worm bin smells.

What is the cheapest way to start composting in an apartment? A DIY worm bin tote for under $40 — see DIY worm bin — plus a $27–$30 countertop bin for daily scraps. Total under $70, and the only ongoing cost is worms.

Do I need a worm bin and a bokashi bucket? Some households run both: the worm bin for fruit and vegetable scraps, the bokashi bucket for meat, dairy, and cooked food. Start with one — the one that matches most of your waste — and add the second only if the first leaves a waste stream unhandled.
