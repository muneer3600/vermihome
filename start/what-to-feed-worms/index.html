What to Feed Worms: The Complete Feeding Guide

Published October 15, 20267 min read

Composting worms (red wigglers, Eisenia fetida) eat fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, and plain paper — and turn them into rich castings for your plants. They do not eat everything, and feeding them the wrong things is the fastest way to get smells, flies, and unhappy worms. This guide tells you exactly what goes in the bin and what stays out.

If you’re still setting up, start with vermicomposting for beginners or build your DIY worm bin first — a new bin needs bedding and a settling-in period before heavy feeding.

What do composting worms eat?

In the wild, worms eat decaying organic matter. In your bin, that means most of your plant-based kitchen scraps:

  • Fruit scraps: banana peels, apple cores, melon rinds, berries, mango skins
  • Vegetable scraps: lettuce, carrot peels, cucumber ends, zucchini, cooked plain vegetables
  • Coffee grounds and paper filters
  • Tea bags (remove staples; avoid glossy or plastic-feeling bags)
  • Crushed eggshells (rinsed and dried — they add grit and balance acidity)
  • Plain bread, cooked rice, and pasta in small amounts
  • Shredded newspaper, cardboard, and paper towels (also used as bedding)

Worms eat roughly half their body weight per day once a bin is established — but a brand-new bin needs two to three weeks to reach that pace. Start small and work up. Our feeding planner helps you match portions to your bin.

When in doubt, leave it out. Worms thrive on boring vegetable scraps; they don’t need variety.

Foods to always avoid

The hard-no list is short but important: meat, dairy, oils, and fats. These don’t just upset worms — they go rancid, smell terrible, and attract pests. In an apartment, that’s the difference between an invisible hobby and a problem your neighbors notice. Pet waste is also off-limits due to pathogens.

Citrus, onions, and garlic sit in a gray zone. Small amounts are fine, but a whole bag of orange peels or a pile of onion skins will make the bin too acidic and the worms will avoid that area. If your bin starts smelling sour, cut these back and add crushed eggshells plus dry cardboard.

How much should I feed?

The classic rule: worms eat about half their body weight per day in an established bin. A pound of worms handles roughly half a pound of scraps daily. But the real answer for beginners is: feed less than you think, and watch the bin.

Practical method:

  1. Start with a small handful of scraps twice a week for the first two to three weeks.
  2. Before each feeding, check the last spot — if food is still there, wait.
  3. Increase gradually as the population grows and food disappears faster.
  4. A settled bin for 1–2 people typically handles 2–4 cups of scraps per week.

Overfeeding is the number one beginner mistake. Uneaten food rots, the bin goes anaerobic, and you get the smell and flies everyone fears. An underfed bin just has patient worms. For a detailed schedule, see how often to feed your worms.

Should I chop or freeze scraps?

Yes to both — they make a real difference.

Chop scraps into small pieces. Worms have no teeth; they eat food as it softens and decays. Smaller pieces break down faster, so worms process them sooner and there’s less sitting around to smell. A quick rough chop while you’re cooking is enough — no need to be precise.

Freeze scraps before feeding. This is the apartment-dweller’s secret weapon. Freezing bursts the cell walls of fruit and vegetables, so scraps soften almost immediately after thawing and worms eat them much faster. Even better: freezing kills fruit fly eggs that arrive on produce skins, which is the main way flies get into indoor bins. Keep a container in the freezer, toss scraps in all week, thaw, and feed.

Thaw frozen scraps before adding them — a frozen lump in the bin chills the worms and adds excess water as it melts. Drain off the liquid if there’s a lot of it.

Try the pocket feeding method

Instead of spreading food across the whole bin, use pocket feeding: dig a small hole in the bedding in one corner, drop the scraps in, and cover them with 2–3 inches of bedding. Next feeding, use a different corner, rotating around the bin.

Why it works:

  • Food is always buried — no exposed scraps, no smell, no flies.
  • You can see exactly how the last feeding is progressing before adding more.
  • It keeps the bin’s surface calm and covered with dry bedding, which worms like.

This single habit prevents most apartment worm-bin problems. If a pocket ever smells off when you open it, that spot was overfed — skip it for a feeding or two and add dry shredded cardboard.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Feeding a brand-new bin like an established one. New worms need time. Patience in month one pays off all year.
  • Burying food too shallow. Two to three inches of bedding cover, minimum. Skimpy cover is how flies find food.
  • Adding liquids. No soups, sauces, or juice. Wet bins go sour — back off on wet scraps and add dry cardboard until it balances.
  • Feeding the “exciting” scraps. Worms don’t need pineapple tops and onion piles. Boring is better.

FAQ

Can worms eat moldy food? Yes — worms actually prefer partially decayed food. A little mold on vegetable scraps is fine and normal in a bin. Just don’t add large quantities of heavily molded food all at once.

Do I need to feed worms every day? No. Two to three feedings per week is plenty for a typical apartment bin. Worms also eat their bedding (cardboard, paper), so they won’t starve between feedings.

Can I feed worms eggshells every time? A small sprinkle of finely crushed, rinsed eggshells with each feeding is ideal. They provide grit for the worms’ digestion and keep acidity in check. Don’t dump in whole shells — crush them fine.

What happens if I accidentally fed something I shouldn’t have? Fish it out if you can. If it’s already buried, don’t panic over a small amount — remove what you can, add extra dry bedding over the spot, and skip the next feeding. One mistake won’t ruin a bin; repeated ones will.
