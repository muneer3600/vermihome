Indoor Worm Bin Setup for Apartments

Published October 15, 20267 min read

An indoor worm bin turns your kitchen scraps into rich compost without a yard, a garden, or any smell — if you set it up right. This guide covers where to put it in a flat, what size works for one or two people, and how to prevent odor and fruit flies from day one.

Most failed apartment worm bins fail for the same two reasons: too much food too soon, and a bin that’s too big or poorly placed. Get those right and the rest is easy. If you haven’t started composting yet, read our beginner guide first, then come back.

Where should I put a worm bin in an apartment?

Worms need a spot that’s dark, room temperature, and easy for you to reach. The ideal range is 55–77°F (13–25°C) — normal indoor conditions. Good options in a flat:

Location Pros Watch out for
Under the kitchen sink Closest to scraps, easy habit Check for leaks and heat from pipes; keep it dark
Hall or coat closet Out of sight, stable temperature Needs airflow — crack the door or drill lid holes
Laundry room / utility nook Hidden, warm, convenient Keep away from the dryer vent (too hot)
Balcony (mild climates only) Out of the way entirely Freezing and summer heat kill worms — bring it inside in extremes

Avoid spots next to radiators, ovens, or sunny windows. Worms tolerate a range of temperatures but die in sustained heat above about 85°F (29°C). In winter, a balcony bin in a cold climate is a death sentence — keep the bin indoors when nights drop near freezing.

A closed bin under the sink or in a closet does not smell if it’s fed correctly. Smell comes from overfeeding and anaerobic (airless) conditions, not from worms themselves.

What size bin do I need?

For one or two people, a bin with about 8–10 gallons (30–40 liters) of volume is plenty — roughly the size of a standard storage tote. That handles the vegetable scraps of a small household without becoming a management burden.

Bigger is not better here. An oversized bin tempts you to dump in too much food, which rots before the worms can process it and causes the exact smell and fly problems apartment dwellers fear. Start small; you can always add a second bin later.

You have two routes:

  1. DIY tote bin. A dark plastic storage tote with air holes drilled in the lid and upper sides, filled with damp shredded cardboard bedding. It’s cheap, effective, and the most common apartment setup. See how to build a DIY worm bin for the full walkthrough.
  2. Commercial stacking tray system. Neater looking, easier harvesting, but costs more. Either works fine — worms don’t care about aesthetics.

Whichever you choose, the bedding matters more than the container. Fill the bin two-thirds full with moist, fluffy bedding: shredded cardboard, coconut coir, or newspaper. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge.

How do I keep it from smelling?

A healthy worm bin smells like forest soil. If yours smells bad, something specific is wrong — usually one of these:

  • Overfeeding. The number one cause. Worms eat roughly half their body weight per day, and a new bin needs weeks to reach full appetite. Start with small amounts and increase gradually. Our worm calculator helps you match worm quantity to your household.
  • Food exposed on top. Always bury food under 2–3 inches of bedding. Exposed scraps rot and stink.
  • Too wet. Excess moisture goes anaerobic and sour. Add dry shredded cardboard and stop adding wet scraps until it balances out.
  • Wrong foods. Meat, dairy, and oily foods go rancid. Stick to vegetable scraps and fruit — full details in what to feed worms.

The preventive routine is simple: feed modestly, bury everything, and keep a thick layer of dry bedding on top.

What about fruit flies?

Fruit flies are the other apartment fear, and they’re almost entirely preventable. They arrive on the skins of fruit you bring home, then breed in exposed scraps.

Prevention from day one:

  • Freeze your scraps. Freezing fruit and vegetable scraps for a day or two kills fruit fly eggs before they ever reach the bin. It also breaks down cell walls so worms eat scraps faster.
  • Bury food deeply. No exposed food on the surface, ever.
  • Keep a dry bedding cap. A 2-inch layer of dry shredded newspaper or cardboard on top blocks flies from reaching food.
  • Harvest ripe fruit promptly. Don’t leave a bowl of overripe bananas on the counter all week.

If flies do show up, a small dish of apple cider vinegar with a drop of dish soap near the bin catches adults while you fix the source. For a full battle plan, search our troubleshooting section for the fruit fly guide.

Do I need to tell my landlord?

This depends on your lease, but here’s a practical way to think about it: a worm bin is a sealed container of bedding and worms, roughly the footprint of a laundry basket, with no smell and no mess. It’s closer to a houseplant than to a pet.

Landlord-friendly talking points if it ever comes up:

  • It’s fully contained — nothing leaks, nothing escapes.
  • There’s no odor when maintained (and you know how to maintain it now).
  • It reduces your household trash, which landlords generally like.
  • It’s temporary and portable — it moves with you like furniture.

Check your lease for any language about composting or “pest attractants,” but most standard leases say nothing that covers a tidy indoor worm bin. If you’re in doubt, the quiet approach works: set it up well, keep it clean, and nobody will ever know it’s there.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Starting with too many worms. A common impulse. Match worms to your actual scrap output — too many worms in a new bin just means hungry worms.
  • Feeding like it’s a garbage disposal. Week one is for bedding and patience, not a full bag of scraps.
  • Forgetting drainage. Even indoor bins need a way for excess moisture to escape or be absorbed. A few drainage holes with a tray underneath, or generous dry bedding, prevents a soggy mess.
  • Placing it and forgetting it. Check the bin twice a week for the first month. After that, it’s a 10-minute weekly routine.

FAQ

Will my neighbors smell it? No. A correctly fed bin smells like damp soil, and only if you stick your head in it. Smell complaints come from overfed, soggy bins — both preventable with the steps above.

Can I keep a worm bin in a studio apartment? Yes. Under the sink or in a closet works in even the smallest studios. The bin is about the size of a storage tote, and a well-run one is invisible and odorless.

What if I go on vacation? Worms are fine unfed for two to three weeks. Give them a normal feeding, add extra moist bedding, and leave. They’re far lower maintenance than a pet.

Is it messy? No. Everything stays inside the bin. The only “mess” is the five minutes of handling scraps during feeding — the same scraps you’d otherwise throw in the trash.
