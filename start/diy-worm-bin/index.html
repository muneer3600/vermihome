DIY Worm Bin: How to Make a Worm Bin for Under $40

Published October 15, 20266 min read

You can build a perfectly good worm bin from a $10 plastic tote in under an hour. No special skills, no expensive kit. This guide gives you the exact materials list, where to drill, how to prepare bedding, and what to do in the critical first week.

If you’re brand new to the whole idea, start with vermicomposting for beginners first.

What you’ll need

Materials

Item Notes Typical cost
10-gallon opaque plastic tote with lid Dark color (worms hate light); ~24”×16”×12” $8–12
Shallow tray or second lid Catches drainage under the bin $0–3
Bedding: cardboard/newspaper or coconut coir About 4–6 lbs dry; see below $0–8
Red wiggler worms, ½–1 lb Order after the bin is ready $30–45
Drill with ¼” bit (or a hot nail + pliers) For ventilation and drainage on hand

Total: roughly $40–65, with worms being most of it. Compare: ready-made stackable worm towers run $80–130.

Bedding options (pick one)

  • Shredded cardboard (best free option): corrugated boxes, tape removed, shredded into 1-inch strips. Avoid glossy/waxy cardboard.
  • Shredded newspaper: black-and-white print is fine; skip glossy inserts.
  • Coconut coir brick: $5–8, expands in water, very consistent. Great if you have no shredder.

Step-by-step build

Step 1: Drill ventilation holes

Worms breathe through their skin and need airflow — but holes must be small enough that worms don’t wander out casually.

  • Lid: 15–20 holes, ¼ inch, spread evenly.
  • Upper sides: one row of 8–10 holes, ¼ inch, about 2 inches below the rim, all the way around.
  • Bottom: 8–10 holes, ¼ inch, for drainage (this is what keeps the bin from going anaerobic and smelly).

No drill? Heat a nail over a flame (hold with pliers), push through the plastic, wiggle slightly. Works fine — ventilate the area.

Step 2: Prepare the bedding

  1. Shred enough cardboard/newspaper to loosely fill the tote two-thirds full.
  2. Soak it in water for 10 minutes, then squeeze out handfuls until each is like a wrung-out sponge — damp, no drips.
  3. Fluff the damp bedding into the bin loosely. Don’t pack it down; air pockets matter.
  4. Toss in a small handful of crushed eggshells or a spoonful of garden lime if you have it (buffers pH — optional but nice).

Step 3: Let it settle (optional but smart)

If you can, let the prepared bin sit for 2–3 days before worms arrive. This lets the bedding’s initial heating settle and gives you time to check that drainage works (no puddle in the tray = good).

Step 4: Add the worms

  1. Dump the worms gently onto the bedding surface.
  2. Leave the lid off with a light shining on the bin for 2–3 hours. Worms hate light and will burrow down into the bedding themselves.
  3. Put the lid on. Place the bin where it will live — see apartment setup for placement.

Step 5: The first week (critical)

  • Don’t feed for 2–3 days. Let worms settle into the bedding.
  • Leave a light on near the bin for the first 2–3 nights. New bins trigger escape attempts; light keeps them burrowing down. (More in worms escaping.)
  • First feeding: a single handful of soft scraps (banana peel, lettuce), buried 2–3 inches deep under bedding in one corner.
  • Check moisture after a few days: bedding surface drying out? Mist lightly. Soggy? Add dry shredded cardboard.

Apartment-specific tips

  • Odor insurance: the drainage tray + correct moisture + burying food = no smell. If your kitchen runs warm, keep the bin in a closet or under the sink rather than next to the oven.
  • Fruit fly shield: from day one, keep 2 inches of dry shredded paper on top as a “mulch cap” and always bury food. This single habit prevents the #1 indoor complaint.
  • Noise/space: the bin is silent and fits in ~2 square feet. Roommates won’t know unless you tell them.

DIY tote vs. bought worm tower

DIY tote (this guide) Stackable worm tower
Cost ~$10 + worms $80–130 + worms
Harvesting ease Manual (dump-and-sort or migration) Easier (tray rotation)
Footprint One tote Taller, smaller footprint
Best for Beginners, budget, testing the hobby Committed composters, tight spaces

Honest take: start with the tote. If you love it after 6 months, upgrade — your worms transfer over in minutes.

Troubleshooting the new bin

Symptom (first 2 weeks) Likely cause Fix
Worms climbing walls New-bin stress (normal) Light on at night for 2–3 days
Condensation on lid Too wet / warm spot Add dry bedding, move to cooler spot
Slight earthy smell Normal None needed
Sour/rotten smell Overfed already or no drainage Stop feeding, add dry bedding, check drain holes

FAQ

What size tote is best?

10 gallons (about 40 liters) suits 1–2 people. A household of 3–4 can use an 18-gallon tote or run two 10-gallon bins. Bigger is more forgiving than smaller.

Do I need to buy a special “worm bin”?

No. Commercial bins are convenient but not better at composting. The worms don’t care about branding.

Can I use a transparent tote?

Not ideal — worms avoid light and a clear bin stresses them. If it’s all you have, wrap it in a dark cloth or keep it inside a closet.

How long does the build take?

About 30–60 minutes including shredding cardboard. The worms usually arrive a few days later by mail — build first, order second.
