Under-Sink Worm Bin: Setup, Size and Smell Guide for Apartments

The cabinet under the kitchen sink is the best spot for an apartment worm bin: dark, warm, steps from the cutting board, and hidden behind a door. A bin that lives under the sink is a bin you’ll actually feed — and one nobody sees.

This guidance is based on widely documented vermicomposting practice, not personal testing. If you haven’t set up a bin at all yet, start with our indoor setup guide — this guide is specifically about making the under-sink spot work.

Why under the sink works so well

Worms want exactly what a sink cabinet provides:

  • Darkness. Worms avoid light — a closed cabinet stays dark all day, so worms stay in the bedding instead of clustering at the lid.
  • Stable temperature. Red wigglers are happiest between roughly 55°F and 77°F (13–25°C), and most kitchens sit in that range year-round — warmer than balconies, steadier than outside-wall closets.
  • Proximity. Scraps go from cutting board to bin in seconds — short distance is the difference between a habit and a chore.
  • Out of sight. Guests, landlords, and roommates never see it. In a studio apartment, this matters more than anywhere else.

The only real downside is space: sink cabinets are small and often crowded with pipes. Measure before you buy or build anything.

Measure the space first

Open the cabinet, clear it out, and measure three numbers before choosing a bin:

Measurement How to take it Why it matters
Width Wall to wall at the cabinet floor The bin must slide in and out for feeding and harvesting
Depth Front edge to the back wall, minus pipe space Pipes eat depth — measure around them, not through them
Height Cabinet floor to the underside of the sink basin Stacking trays need vertical room; the lid needs clearance to open

Leave at least 2 inches of clearance on each side — room to lift the lid, pull the bin out, and reach the drain pipes without moving the setup. A bin that fits exactly is a bin you’ll dread touching.

Check the cabinet floor too: some have a raised lip or a cutout around pipes. A small plywood base levels it and protects it from drips.

Which bin types fit under a sink

Two designs work. Pick by your cabinet’s height.

Stacking tray systems suit tall cabinets: trays stack upward, worms migrate up to fresh food, and you harvest from the bottom tray. They need height for 3–4 trays plus lid clearance.

Single totes suit short or wide cabinets: one shallow opaque tote with a lid and drilled air holes holds a full colony, slides in and out easily, and is the simplest to harvest from — tip, sort, and done. A tote 12–18 inches long fits most cabinets.

Avoid flow-through bags under a sink: they need to hang, and cabinets don’t give them the clearance or airflow they want. For a full comparison of bin types, see how to choose a worm bin.

Size by household, not by cabinet: 1–2 people need roughly 10–15 gallons of bedding volume. Pick the largest bin that still leaves you working room — not the largest that fits.

Ventilation under a closed cabinet

A closed cabinet can get stuffy, and worms need air. Three things keep airflow healthy:

  1. Drill air holes — about ¼ inch — along the upper sides and lid. They let air in without letting worms out; cover them with fine mesh if flies worry you.
  2. Don’t seal the cabinet. Leave the door ajar an hour or two a few times a week. Fresh air prevents the stale, humid pocket that causes smells.
  3. Keep bedding fluffy, not packed. Loose shredded cardboard lets air move; fluff matted bedding with a fork at each feeding.

If you open the cabinet and the air smells stale, that’s your signal: more ventilation, less water, and a dry cardboard cap on top of the bedding.

Leak protection: the tray underneath

Worm bins can drip — usually a little leachate from the bottom, especially in the first weeks or if the bin gets too wet. Under a sink, a drip lands on the cabinet floor and rots it. Prevention is one cheap item:

  • Put the bin in a shallow tray — a boot tray, a rimmed baking sheet, or a plastic storage lid. It catches drips; empty it when you feed.
  • Don’t overwater. Bedding should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If water pools in the tray after every feeding, you’re adding too much wet food — back off and add dry cardboard.
  • Check the trap and pipes monthly. Sink cabinets hide slow drain leaks, and opening the cabinet often means you’ll spot one early.

If leachate does collect, drain it off and discard it — it’s not worm tea, and it can smell. Real worm tea is brewed from finished castings; see worm tea vs leachate for the difference.

Keep cleaning products away from worms

This is the under-sink spot’s one real danger. Sink cabinets hold dish soap, bleach, drain cleaner, and sprays — and all of them are lethal to worms.

  • Move chemicals to a different cabinet if you can. A high shelf or the cabinet above the sink is safer.
  • If they must share the cabinet, keep them on the opposite side, upright with tight lids, in a plastic tub so a spill can’t reach the bin.
  • Never spray anything inside the cabinet. Air freshener, bug spray, and cleaning sprays drift onto the bedding and into the bin’s air holes.
  • Wash your hands after handling chemicals before touching the bin or feeding.

One splash of bleach or drain cleaner can kill a colony overnight. This is the single most important rule for under-sink bins.

Smell control in an enclosed space

A healthy bin smells like fresh soil — but an enclosed cabinet concentrates any smell, so the margin is smaller. Three habits keep it at zero:

  1. Always bury food. Pocket-feed with 2–3 inches of cover. Exposed food is the number one cause of bin smell — see why your bin smells if yours already does.
  2. Keep a dry cap. A thick layer of dry shredded cardboard on top absorbs moisture and odor. Replace it when it gets damp.
  3. Feed less than you think. Overfeeding causes the sour, anaerobic smell that fills a cabinet. A new bin needs small feedings for the first month — check the last pocket before adding more.

If the cabinet smells when you open it, don’t mask it — fix it. Pull out the bin, check for uneaten food and wet pockets, add dry bedding, and skip a feeding. The smell clears in a day or two once the cause is gone.

FAQ

Will a worm bin under the sink attract cockroaches? A healthy bin with buried food and a tight lid doesn’t attract cockroaches — they want exposed food and standing water, which a well-run bin doesn’t offer. If roaches are already in the kitchen, fix that separately; the bin isn’t the cause. For other pests, see our rats and mice guide.

Can I put the bin under a bathroom sink instead? You can, but the kitchen is better: it’s warmer, closer to food scraps, and you open it daily. A bathroom cabinet works if the kitchen has no space — just keep cleaning products far away and make sure the cabinet stays above 55°F.

How often do I need to open the cabinet? Feeding takes a few minutes, two to three times a week, plus a weekly check for moisture and dry bedding. Low-maintenance, not no-maintenance.

What if my sink cabinet is too small for any bin? Measure again — many cabinets fit a shallow 10-gallon tote even when a tray system won’t. If nothing fits, a closet shelf or a spot beside the fridge works the same way. The key is dark, warm, and reachable — not the sink specifically.
