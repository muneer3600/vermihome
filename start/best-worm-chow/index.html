The best worm chow for most apartment worm bins is Uncle Jim’s Worm Farm Organic Worm Food, 2.5 lb, at about $19.95. It is a powdered grain-based feed made by a large US worm farm, it feeds about 500 worms for roughly a month, and a listing mirror records 339 ratings averaging 4.6 out of 5. If you want the cheapest way to supplement your worms, the Worm Feed and Grit sold on Etsy at $9.95 does the same basic job — food plus grit — for half the price.

Do you even need to buy worm food? Honest answer: no, not always. Kitchen scraps are free worm food, and most apartment bins run fine on scraps alone (see what to feed worms and how often to feed worms). Bought worm chow earns its place in three situations: when you are starting a new bin and have few scraps yet, when you want faster worm breeding, and when you travel and need a slow, dry food that will not rot while you are away. Think of it as a supplement, not a replacement for scraps.

1. Uncle Jim’s Worm Food, 2.5 lb — best overall

Uncle Jim’s is the biggest name in US vermicomposting, and this is the same powdered feed the farm uses on its own worm beds, according to the product listing. The recipe is grain-based and organic, designed to support digestion, reproduction, and richer castings. One 2.5 lb bag is sold as a one-month supply for about 500 composting worms, which matches a typical apartment bin stocked with half a pound of red wigglers.

At about $19.95 on Amazon (via a price-tracking listing), it works out to roughly $8 per pound — not cheap per pound, but a little goes a long way: you sprinkle a small handful on the bedding once or twice a week, not a cup a day. The listing mirror records 339 ratings averaging 4.6 out of 5, with buyers reporting fast growth and worms eating it readily. A 7.5 lb three-month bag also exists, but we could not verify a current US price for it on 6 November 2026 — a non-US marketplace listed it at $216.48, which is clearly an import markup, not a US price. Check the listing for your size before you order.

Best for: most apartment bins. If you buy one bag of worm food, buy this one.

2. Worm Feed and Grit, 2 or 5 lb — best budget pick

This Etsy-sold supplement is the cheapest named worm food we found, at $9.95 for a 2 or 5 lb bag (the listing does not make clear which size the $9.95 covers, so confirm the size at checkout). It combines feed with grit in one bag, which matters: worms have gizzards like chickens and need grit — sand, crushed eggshell, or rock dust — to grind their food. A feed without grit is only half a product.

The trade-off is transparency. Unlike Uncle Jim’s, there is no published ingredient list from a large farm behind it, and no rating count we could verify from a live source. It is a small-seller product, so quality may vary between batches. For the price, though, it is the lowest-risk way to try bought worm food: if your worms ignore it, you have lost ten dollars, not twenty.

Best for: beginners on a tight budget, and anyone who wants feed and grit in a single bag.

3. Premium 7-Ingredient Worm Chow + Grit, 8 oz — best for small bins

This Etsy listing is a small-batch chow: seven ingredients in a real vegetable blend, with grit included, at $14.99 for 8 oz. That is expensive per pound — about $30 per pound — but the bag is small on purpose. An 8 oz bag is the right size for a single small apartment bin, where a 2.5 lb bag would sit in a cupboard for a year and go stale.

Small-batch chow like this is also the best choice if you keep European nightcrawlers as well as red wigglers, since the vegetable blend suits both. The downside, again, is the small seller: no verified rating count, and $14.99 buys you only half a pound. Buy it for a small bin, not for a big one.

Best for: studio apartments, single-tray bins, and anyone who wants a small fresh bag rather than a bulk sack.

4. NEW ERA Gardener Worm Food Chow, 2 lb — best bulk organic mix

NEW ERA Gardener’s modified worm food chow is a 2 lb powdered mix sold on eBay-style marketplaces at about $19.95 Buy It Now. The published ingredient list is the most detailed of any chow in this guide: organic corn, organic soybean meal, organic barley, organic oats, organic wheat middlings, organic flax meal, and organic cane molasses, plus alfalfa meal, kelp, rock dust, and steamed ground eggshells for grit. That is a complete feed and grit in one bag, and every ingredient is named.

The seller’s own note says worms eat it readily and that it is useful before a harvest, when you want remaining food eaten so the castings are clean. The catch is the channel: it is sold through marketplace listings rather than a brand storefront, so availability and price vary by listing — another listing for the same product showed $17.95. Treat $19.95 as typical, and check the listing you actually buy from.

Best for: buyers who want a fully organic ingredient list and a 2 lb bag that lasts months.

5. Kitchen scraps + crushed eggshell — best free option

The best worm food for most people costs nothing: your kitchen scraps. Vegetable peels, fruit ends, coffee grounds, tea bags (staples removed), and torn cardboard are all worm food already in your kitchen. Add crushed eggshell for grit and pH balance — rinse the shells, bake them at low heat for 20 minutes, then grind them to a fine powder. That is exactly the grit worms need, and it costs nothing.

Free feeding has one real weakness: consistency. Scraps vary by week, and a bin fed only on watermelon rinds one week and only coffee grounds the next will not breed as fast as a bin with a steady powdered supplement. If your goal is maximum worm population — for example, you plan to split the bin or sell worms — a bought chow helps. If your goal is simply to compost your scraps, free food is enough, and this guide’s bought picks are optional.

Best for: everyone. Start here, and only buy chow if you have a specific reason.

How much worm chow to feed

A common beginner mistake is treating chow like scraps and dumping in a cup at a time. Powdered feed is concentrated. Start with one to two tablespoons sprinkled on damp bedding, once or twice a week, and watch whether it disappears within a few days. If it is gone, feed a little more next time. If it sits there, feed less — uneaten chow goes mouldy, and mouldy feed attracts fungus gnats.

Always bury chow under a thin layer of bedding. Food left on the surface dries out or draws fruit flies. And always pair chow with grit if the chow does not include it: a pinch of crushed eggshell or a spoonful of fine sand with each feeding. Worms cannot grind food without grit, so chow without grit is wasted money.

What not to buy

  • A 7.5 lb bag for a single small bin. It will go stale before you finish it. Buy the 2.5 lb or 8 oz size instead.
  • Mealworm food labelled as worm chow. Mealworms and composting worms eat different things; mealworm bedding is usually wheat bran, which is fine as an ingredient but is not a complete worm chow on its own.
  • Any feed that does not list its ingredients. If the seller will not say what is in it, do not put it in your bin. Salt, onion powder, and citrus oils are all harmful to worms and can hide in unnamed blends.

FAQ

Do worms need bought food, or are scraps enough? Scraps are enough for composting. Bought chow helps when you want faster breeding, when you are starting a new bin, or when you travel and need a dry food that will not rot.

How long does a 2.5 lb bag last? About a month for 500 worms at the maker’s suggested rate, and two to three months for a typical apartment bin fed once or twice a week.

Can I feed worms only chow and no scraps? You can, but you should not. Scraps provide moisture and variety that dry chow does not. Use chow as a supplement alongside scraps, not a replacement.

What is grit, and why do worms need it? Worms have a gizzard that grinds food, and it needs hard particles to work — crushed eggshell, fine sand, or rock dust. Without grit, worms cannot digest food efficiently, however good the chow is.
