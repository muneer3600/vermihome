How to Set Up a Store-Bought Worm Bin: Your First 30 Days

Your worm bin will arrive in a box, and your worms will arrive in a separate box, probably on a different day. That is normal. Set the bin up first, let the bedding settle for a day, and add the worms when they arrive. If you do those three things in that order — bin, bedding, worms — the first 30 days will go smoothly. If you put worms into dry bedding with a pile of food on top, they will try to leave, and you will conclude that worm composting does not work. It does. The order matters.

This guide covers a store-bought bin: a tray stack like the Worm Factory 360 or VermiHut Plus, or a flow-through bag like the Urban Worm Bag. If you built a DIY tote instead, the steps are the same except for the tray parts — see DIY worm bin for the tote version. If you have not chosen a bin yet, read how to choose a worm bin first.

Before the bin arrives: what you need

You need four things, and you probably have three of them already.

  • Bedding. Shredded cardboard, shredded newspaper, or coconut coir. If your bin came with coir bricks, use those. If it did not, shredded cardboard from delivery boxes works — remove tape and glossy print first. You need enough to fill the first tray about three-quarters full once it is damp and fluffed. Budget about $10 for coir if you buy it.
  • Water. Plain tap water is fine. If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, let it sit uncovered for a day before you use it.
  • A small amount of food. Not a week’s worth — a single handful for the first feeding. Vegetable peelings, fruit scraps, coffee grounds, and crushed eggshells are good. No meat, dairy, oil, or citrus in the first month. See what to feed worms for the full list.
  • A tray or mat under the bin. A boot mat, a baking tray, or a piece of plastic. A new bin can drip a little while the bedding settles, and a mat prevents the only real mess a worm bin makes.

Day 1: set up the bin and the bedding

Put the bin where it will live — under the sink, in a closet, in a kitchen corner — and do not plan to move it often. Worms like a stable spot at roughly 55 to 80°F, the same range that suits your living room. Avoid a hot balcony, a freezing garage, or a spot next to a radiator.

Assemble the bin according to its instructions. For a tray stack, that means the base with its drainage tray, one working tray, and the lid. Do not stack all five trays on day one. You need one working tray. The other trays stay in the cupboard until the colony grows into them.

Prepare the bedding. If you are using coir, soak the brick in water until it expands, then squeeze out the excess until it is damp like a wrung-out sponge. If you are using shredded cardboard or newspaper, soak it, then squeeze it the same way. Fill the working tray about three-quarters full with damp, fluffed bedding. Do not pack it down — worms need air in the bedding, and packed bedding goes airless.

Add a handful of crushed eggshells or a little grit if you have it. Worms have no teeth; grit helps them grind food. Eggshells also help balance acidity. This is optional in week one, but it is a good habit.

Leave the lid on and the bedding alone for a day if your worms have not arrived yet. Damp bedding with no worms in it will not smell. It will just sit there, ready.

If your worms have already arrived, add them now — see the next step — and then leave the bin alone.

Day 1–2: add the worms, and use the light trick

When the worms arrive, open the box straight away. Do not leave it in a mailbox or a parcel locker. The worms will be in moist bedding inside a bag or a sack. Tip them gently onto the surface of the bedding in the working tray, bedding and all. Do not spread them out or bury them. They will burrow down on their own within an hour or two.

Now use the new-bin light trick. Leave a light on over the open bin for the first night — a desk lamp, or the kitchen light. Worms avoid light, so the light tells them to stay down in the bedding instead of exploring upward and out of the bin. By morning, they will have settled. Put the lid on and turn the light off. You only need to do this for the first night or two, while the worms are new to the bin. After that, the lid and the bedding are enough.

Do not feed the worms on the day they arrive. They have bedding to eat, and they need a day to settle. Feeding on day one is the most common new-bin mistake: the food sits uneaten, the bedding gets wet, and the bin smells before it has even started. Wait.

If any worms are still on the surface or trying to climb the walls after two days, something is wrong with the bedding — usually it is too wet, too dry, or too acidic. Check the squeeze test first. If bedding drips when you squeeze it, add dry shredded cardboard. If it feels dry and crumbly, sprinkle water lightly. If worms keep escaping after that, read worms escaping — it covers the other causes, including a bin that is too warm or bedding that was never soaked properly.

Week 1: the first feeding

On day three or four, give the worms their first food: a single handful of scraps, buried under the bedding in one corner of the tray. Burying matters. Food left on the surface attracts fruit flies. Food buried under bedding does not. Push the bedding aside, put the food in, and cover it again.

Then leave the bin alone for a week. Do not dig it up to check. Do not add more food because you are worried the worms are hungry. They are not hungry — they have a whole tray of bedding to eat, and a small colony eats slowly at first. A pound of worms eats about half a pound of food a day at full speed, but a new colony in a new bin eats much less than that while it settles.

At the end of week one, check the food. Lift the bedding in the corner where you buried it. If most of it is gone, you can feed again — another handful, buried in a different corner. If most of it is still there, wait another few days. Rotating the feeding spot around the tray is the whole feeding method: it spreads the worms out and stops one corner from going sour.

If fruit flies appear in week one, you are almost certainly leaving food on the surface or feeding too much. Bury the food, add a dry bedding cap on top, and read fruit flies in your worm bin for the trap that clears them in days.

Week 2: settle into a routine

By week two, the bin should smell like fresh earth when you lift the lid, and the worms should be in the bedding, not on the walls. Your routine from here is simple, and it stays the same for the life of the bin.

  • Feed once or twice a week. A handful of scraps, buried, in a rotating spot. See how often to feed worms for the full routine.
  • Check moisture once a week. Squeeze a handful of bedding. A drop or two is right. Dripping is too wet — add dry cardboard. Crumbly and dry is too dry — sprinkle water. See too wet or too dry.
  • Leave the lid on. The lid keeps moisture in and flies out. Take it off only to feed or check.

Do not add a second tray in week two. A new colony has not finished the first tray, and a second tray on top of unfinished food just gives the worms somewhere to escape to while the bottom tray goes sour. Patience here saves you a smelly bin later.

If the bin smells bad in week two, stop feeding for a week and add dry shredded cardboard. Do not add more food to a smelly bin — that is like feeding a sick person a feast. The full fix is in why your worm bin smells.

Week 3: the colony starts to work

In week three, you should see the first signs that the system is working. The bedding will be darker and finer than when you started. The food you buried in week one should be mostly gone. The worms should be spread through the tray rather than clustered in one corner.

This is the week to start noticing how much your household actually produces. If you are feeding twice a week and the food is gone in two days, your colony is ready for more. If you are feeding once a week and food is still there after five days, you are feeding too much. Adjust the amount, not the bin.

Week 4: check, and decide what is next

At the end of the first 30 days, do a proper check. Lift the lid and look at the tray. The bedding should be dark, moist but not wet, and smell like earth. The worms should be active and spread out. Most of the food you buried should be gone within a few days of feeding.

If that is what you see, the bin is working. Your job from here is the same routine: feed once or twice a week, check moisture weekly, and add a tray when the first one is nearly finished. Our indoor worm bin setup guide covers the long-term routine in full.

If the bin smells, or the bedding is soaking wet, or worms are still escaping after a month, something needs fixing — and it is fixable. Work through the troubleshooting hub in order: smell first, then moisture, then escaping. Most month-one problems are overfeeding, and the fix is a week without food plus dry cardboard.

FAQ

Should I feed my worms on the day they arrive? No. Wait three to four days. The worms need time to settle into the bedding, and they have bedding to eat. Feeding on day one is the most common cause of a smelly new bin.

Why are my worms trying to escape in the first two days? Usually the bedding is too wet, too dry, or too acidic — or the worms are simply new and unsettled. Use the light trick for the first night, check the squeeze test, and give them two days. If they are still escaping after that, see worms escaping.

When do I add a second tray? When the first tray is nearly full of dark, finished-looking material and the worms are crowded at the top — usually after six weeks or more, not in the first month. Adding a tray early leaves unfinished food to go sour underneath.

How long until I get worm castings? A first finished tray usually takes two to three months. A flow-through bag takes longer, because the bottom needs months to finish. The first 30 days are about settling the colony, not harvesting.
