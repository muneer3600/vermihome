A DIY worm bin costs about $15–$30 to build, while a store-bought worm bin costs $69–$140 before worms. Over the first year, the DIY route totals roughly $60–$90 all-in, and the store-bought route totals roughly $120–$200 all-in. DIY wins on price by a wide margin. Store-bought wins on time, looks, and harvesting ease. Which one you should choose depends on whether you have more spare money or more spare time — and this guide prices both honestly, component by component.

Prices below were checked on 6 November 2026 from retailer and roundup listings. Prices change often, so check the seller’s page before you order. We have not hands-on tested these products. This comparison is based on published specifications, seller listings, and published reviews.

What a DIY bin actually costs

A DIY worm bin is a plastic tote or a stack of buckets with air holes drilled in it. That is the whole design, and it works. Here is the honest component pricing, checked 6 November 2026.

The container. A standard 5-gallon HDX bucket costs $5.98 at Home Depot, and the classic orange Homer bucket costs $4.48 without a lid, or $6.66–$9.45 with a lid, according to a January 2026 price comparison. A two- or three-bucket system therefore costs about $12–$19 in buckets and lids. A plastic storage tote is the other classic DIY container: an 8-pack of 18-gallon Sterilite totes lists at $37.96, or about $4.75 each, but we could not verify a separate single-tote price on 6 November 2026, so treat a single tote as roughly $5–$10 and check the listing.

Tools. You need a drill and a drill bit to make air holes and drainage holes. If you already own a drill, the tool cost is $0. If you do not, borrowing one is free and buying a basic drill is $25–$40 — which changes the maths completely, so borrow one. You also need a tray or lid underneath to catch liquid, which is usually a spare tote lid or a baking tray you already own.

Bedding. Free if you use shredded cardboard (see best bedding for worm bins), or about $14.99 for a 1.4 lb Urban Worm coir brick, or $28.99 for a 10 lb Coco Bliss brick that lasts for several re-beds.

Worms. This is the cost people forget, and it is the same whichever route you take. Red wigglers are not free. An Amazon listing snippet recorded Uncle Jim’s 1,000-count red wigglers at $54.95, an Etsy listing showed 100 red wigglers at $12.00, and another Etsy listing showed red wiggler compost worms at $40.00. A typical apartment bin needs about half a pound — roughly 500 worms — so budget $40–$55 for worms from a reputable seller. Cheap worms from an unknown seller are a false economy: dead or wrong-species worms cost you the bin.

DIY first-year total: roughly $60–$90, including worms and bedding, assuming you borrow a drill and use free cardboard bedding. Add $15–$29 if you buy coir bedding, and $25–$40 if you must buy a drill.

What a store-bought bin actually costs

Store-bought bins cost more up front, but the price includes design you cannot easily DIY: stacking trays, airflow channels, a spigot for worm tea, and a lid that actually fits.

VermiHut Plus, 5-tray: $69–$82. Amazon lists it at $69.00, while a November 2026 roundup lists it at $82.00. It is the cheapest proper stacking bin, with five trays, enhanced airflow, and accessories included. Worms are not included, so add $40–$55. First-year total: roughly $115–$150.

Worm Factory 360, 4-tray: about $134.95. A November 2026 roundup lists it at $134.95, and another seller lists it at $139.99. The version with worms included costs $171.95 from the maker’s own store — that is the honest all-in price for a ready-to-go system, since it includes half a pound of red wigglers with a redemption voucher. First-year total: roughly $180–$205 without the worm bundle, or $171.95 plus bedding with it.

Urban Worm Bag V2: $139–$139.99. EarthEasy lists it at $139.00 and Uncle Jim’s lists it at $139.99. It is a continuous-flow fabric bin: you add food at the top and harvest castings from the bottom without disturbing the worms. First-year total: roughly $185–$210 with worms and bedding.

What the store price buys you. Stacking trays make harvesting dramatically easier: worms migrate up to fresh food, leaving finished castings behind in the lower tray. A DIY tote has no trays, so harvesting means hand-sorting worms from castings — slow, messy, and the main reason beginners quit. Store bins also look like furniture rather than a storage tote, which matters if the bin lives in your kitchen or living room rather than a balcony.

The hidden costs both routes share

Worms: $40–$55. Same either way, and the single biggest cost after the bin itself.

Bedding: $0–$29. Same either way. Free cardboard works for both.

Feeding: $0–$20 a year. Kitchen scraps are free. Bought worm chow (see best worm chow) costs about $10–$20 a bag and is optional.

Replacement parts: $0–$20. DIY bins need a new tote every year or two as plastic cracks. Store bins need replacement trays only if you break one. Neither is expensive, but DIY replacement is more frequent.

Your time. A DIY bin takes 30–60 minutes to build, plus a trip to the hardware store. A store bin takes 15 minutes to assemble. Harvesting from a DIY tote takes an hour or more each time; harvesting from a stacking tray takes 15 minutes. Over a year, the store bin saves you several hours — which is worth more than the price difference if your time is scarce.

Which should you choose?

Choose DIY if: your budget is under $100, you already own a drill, you do not mind a tote in the corner, and you are happy to hand-sort castings at harvest. The DIY tote is the best way to find out whether you actually enjoy vermicomposting before spending real money.

Choose store-bought if: you want harvesting to be easy, the bin will sit somewhere visible, you have no drill, or you know you will quit if the first harvest is a messy chore. The VermiHut Plus at $69–$82 is the sweet spot: proper stacking trays for little more than a DIY build once worms are included.

Choose the Worm Factory 360 with worms ($171.95) if: you want the most foolproof start possible — bin, worms, bedding, and accessories in one box, with nothing else to buy or build.

What not to do

  • Do not buy the cheapest bin you can find on a marketplace with no brand name. Unbranded stacking bins often have trays that do not fit, lids that warp, and no replacement parts. The $69 VermiHut is the floor for a bin worth owning.
  • Do not skip the worms budget. A $15 DIY bin with no worms is a box of wet cardboard. Budget $40–$55 for worms in either route.
  • Do not drill too few air holes. A DIY bin with poor airflow goes anaerobic and smells. Drill holes in the lid, the sides near the top, and the bottom for drainage — and put a tray underneath.

FAQ

Is a DIY worm bin as good as a store-bought one? For the worms, yes — they do not care what the container looks like. For you, no — harvesting is harder and the bin looks worse. The worms will be equally happy either way.

How long does a DIY tote bin last? One to two years before the plastic cracks or warps. A store-bought bin lasts five years or more with normal use.

Can I upgrade from DIY to store-bought later? Yes, and it is a good plan: start DIY for $60–$90, and if you are still composting in six months, move the worms and bedding into a stacking bin. Nothing is wasted.

What is the absolute cheapest way to start? A two-bucket system at about $12–$19, free cardboard bedding, and 100 red wigglers at $12.00 from Etsy — about $25–$35 total. But 100 worms is a very small colony, so expect slow composting for the first few months.
